# MIS Reporting — 30 Interview Questions

> For MIS Executive interviews — practical reporting knowledge tested at all levels

---

**1. What is MIS and what does an MIS Executive do?**

MIS stands for Management Information System — a system that collects, processes, and presents data to help management make informed decisions. An MIS Executive is responsible for building, maintaining, and distributing reports (daily, weekly, monthly) that track business performance across departments. The role requires strong Excel skills, data accuracy, and an understanding of business KPIs. An MIS Executive acts as the bridge between raw operational data and management-level insights.

---

**2. What are the types of MIS reports?**

MIS reports are broadly classified into three types: Operational reports (day-to-day tracking — daily production, attendance, calls handled), Tactical reports (weekly/fortnightly — performance vs. target, trend analysis), and Strategic reports (monthly/quarterly — high-level KPIs, P&L summaries, business reviews). Each type serves a different audience — operational reports go to team leads, tactical to managers, and strategic to senior leadership.

---

**3. What is a Daily Operations Report?**

A Daily Operations Report is a snapshot of key operational metrics for a single day, typically shared every morning with team leads and managers. It usually includes metrics like calls handled, tickets resolved, production output, attendance, TAT compliance, and revenue generated. The report must be concise, accurate, and delivered on time — usually before the business day begins. It is one of the most important recurring responsibilities of an MIS Executive.

---

**4. What does a Weekly MIS Report typically include?**

A Weekly MIS Report provides a 7-day performance summary and is used for team-level review meetings. It typically includes: daily trend charts, weekly KPI vs. target comparison, top/bottom performer analysis, SLA adherence, exception highlights, and a brief commentary. It bridges the gap between day-to-day operations and monthly strategic reviews. The data is usually presented in a formatted Excel or PowerPoint deck.

---

**5. What is a Monthly MIS Report?**

A Monthly MIS Report is a comprehensive summary of all key business metrics for the month, submitted to senior management. It includes revenue performance, headcount, attrition, SLA compliance, cost data, and comparisons with the previous month and year-ago period. Monthly reports often include MoM and YoY growth analysis, charts, and executive commentary. This report is typically used in business review meetings (BRMs).

---

**6. What is an Exception Report?**

An Exception Report highlights only those records or metrics that fall outside a defined threshold or expected range — it filters out normal data and focuses attention on anomalies. For example, an exception report might show only employees with attendance below 75% or customers with overdue payments beyond 30 days. It saves management time by surfacing only what needs action. Exception reports are often automated using Excel filters, conditional formatting, or macros.

---

**7. What is a Drill-Down Report?**

A Drill-down report allows users to start with a high-level summary and click through to view progressively more detailed data. For example, a sales summary might show total revenue by region; drilling down reveals revenue by city, then by product, then by individual rep. In Excel, this is achieved through pivot tables, hyperlinks, or multiple interconnected sheets. Drill-down reports are powerful for root-cause analysis.

---

**8. What is an Ad-Hoc Report?**

An Ad-hoc report is a one-time, on-demand report created in response to a specific business question that is not covered by standard recurring reports. For example, a manager may ask: "Show me all customers who bought Product X in the last 3 months but haven't reordered." An MIS Executive must be skilled at quickly building such reports from raw data. Ad-hoc reporting requires strong Excel querying skills (VLOOKUP, filters, pivot tables).

---

**9. What is Data Storytelling in MIS reporting?**

Data storytelling is the practice of presenting data findings in a narrative format — combining charts, context, and commentary to guide the audience toward a clear insight or decision. Instead of dumping a table of numbers, an MIS Executive explains what the data means: "Revenue dropped 15% in Week 3 due to a 2-day system outage — it has since recovered." Good data storytelling makes reports actionable, not just informational. It is a skill that distinguishes excellent MIS professionals.

---

**10. How should a management report be structured?**

A well-structured management report follows this order: (1) Executive Summary — 3–5 bullet points of top-level findings, (2) Key KPIs vs. targets with status indicators, (3) Trend charts showing performance over time, (4) Highlights and lowlights, (5) Root cause for any deviation, and (6) Recommended actions. Starting with the executive summary ensures senior leaders get the key message even if they don't read the full report. Always use consistent formatting and avoid jargon.

---

**11. What is data validation in reporting?**

Data validation is the process of verifying that data is accurate, complete, consistent, and within expected ranges before it is used in a report. In Excel, this includes checking for blanks, duplicates, out-of-range values, and formula errors. For example, validating that sales figures match the source system before publishing a revenue report. Without data validation, reports can mislead management and erode trust in the MIS function.

---

**12. How do you handle missing data in reports?**

Missing data should never be ignored or silently replaced with zeros without disclosure. Best practices include: identifying the source of missing data, using a placeholder ("N/A" or "Data Awaited"), adding a footnote explaining the gap, and following up with the data owner. For minor gaps, previous period data or averages may be used as estimates — clearly labeled as such. Transparency about data gaps is critical to maintaining report credibility.

---

**13. What is a Pivot-Based MIS Report?**

A pivot-based MIS report is built around one or more Excel PivotTables that summarize large raw datasets into meaningful breakdowns. For example, raw transaction data can be pivoted to show sales by region, by product, and by month — all in seconds. Pivot tables allow MIS Executives to quickly slice and dice data without writing complex formulas. Connecting slicers to multiple pivot tables creates an interactive dashboard.

---

**14. How do you present negative trends without alarming management?**

Present negative trends with context, root cause, and a recovery plan — never just the bad number in isolation. For example, instead of "CSAT dropped to 68%," say "CSAT dropped to 68% in Week 2 due to a product quality issue that has since been resolved; recovery is expected by Week 4." Use visual cues like arrows and color codes (red/amber/green) so the severity is immediately clear. Always accompany a negative trend with the action being taken.

---

**15. What is a Flash Report?**

A Flash Report is a very short, high-priority summary of the most critical KPIs, typically sent daily or in real-time to senior management. It contains only 5–10 key numbers — no charts, no tables — designed to be read in under 2 minutes. Flash reports are common in sales organizations (daily revenue vs. target) and operations (tickets resolved, SLA breaches). They are often sent via email or messaging tools in a fixed format.

---

**16. What is the difference between a Dashboard and a Report?**

A Dashboard is a visual, real-time or near-real-time display of KPIs and metrics, designed for quick monitoring. A Report is a structured document that presents historical data, analysis, and commentary for a specific period. Dashboards are interactive and updated frequently; reports are static, scheduled, and more detailed. For example, a live call center dashboard shows current queue; a weekly report analyzes the week's performance trends.

---

**17. What information goes in an Executive Summary of a report?**

An executive summary should include: the reporting period, the 3–5 most important performance highlights, major deviations from targets with brief explanations, and any critical risks or recommended actions. It should be written in plain language, not data-heavy — senior leaders want insights, not raw numbers. Limit it to one page or 5 bullet points. The executive summary is often the only section that senior leaders read in detail.

---

**18. How do you maintain report accuracy?**

Report accuracy is maintained through: using a single validated data source, locking formulas with cell protection, building in automated cross-checks (e.g., totals reconciliation), having a peer review before submission, and maintaining a change log. Always reconcile totals against source system figures (e.g., ERP or CRM). Documenting the data source, extraction date, and any assumptions in the report footer protects the MIS Executive when numbers are questioned.

---

**19. What are common data quality issues in MIS?**

Common data quality issues include: duplicate records, missing values, inconsistent formats (e.g., dates in different formats), incorrect data entry (typos, wrong codes), and stale/outdated data. These issues lead to inaccurate reports and poor business decisions. An MIS Executive must build data cleaning steps into their workflow — using Excel tools like Remove Duplicates, Text to Columns, TRIM, and IFERROR functions.

---

**20. How do you build a weekly operational tracker?**

To build a weekly operational tracker: (1) Identify the 8–12 KPIs to track, (2) Create a data input sheet linked to a summary dashboard, (3) Add a trend section showing the last 4–6 weeks, (4) Apply conditional formatting for RAG (Red-Amber-Green) status, (5) Add a commentary column for each metric, and (6) Protect formulas so data entry cells are the only editable areas. The tracker should be templated so it requires minimal rework each week.

---

**21. What is SLA tracking and how is it reported?**

SLA (Service Level Agreement) tracking monitors whether committed service timelines are being met. In a report, SLA performance is shown as: SLA Compliance % = (Tasks resolved within SLA / Total tasks) × 100, along with a breakdown of SLA breaches by category, team, or reason. An SLA tracker typically includes columns for: ticket ID, received time, resolution time, SLA deadline, and breach status (Yes/No). Trends and breach root causes are highlighted in weekly reports.

---

**22. How do you create a Headcount Report?**

A headcount report tracks the number of employees in an organization by department, grade, location, or employment type at a given point in time. It typically includes: opening headcount, joiners, leavers, and closing headcount for the period — a standard "headcount movement" table. Data is sourced from HRMS or the HR team's records. It is a core component of any monthly HR MIS pack submitted to management.

---

**23. What is an Attrition Report?**

An attrition report tracks employee exits over a period, showing total separations, attrition rate by department, voluntary vs. involuntary exits, and trends over time. It is built using HR data (exit records, joining date, department, designation). The report helps HR and management identify high-attrition departments and take retention action. Formula: Attrition Rate = (Leavers / Average Headcount) × 100, reported monthly and cumulatively for the year.

---

**24. What are common Excel templates used in MIS?**

Common MIS Excel templates include: Daily Flash Report, Weekly Operations Tracker, Monthly KPI Dashboard, Headcount & Attrition Report, SLA Compliance Tracker, Revenue vs. Target Report, and Incentive Calculation Sheet. These templates are typically pre-formatted with formulas, conditional formatting, and drop-down lists — only the raw data needs to be updated. Maintaining a clean template library is a key responsibility of an MIS Executive.

---

**25. How do you automate repetitive MIS tasks?**

Repetitive MIS tasks can be automated using: Excel Macros/VBA (auto-format, auto-copy, auto-email), Power Query (auto-refresh data connections), Excel formulas that auto-calculate when data is pasted, and scheduled task scripts. For example, a macro can format a raw data dump into a formatted report in seconds. Automation reduces errors, saves time, and allows the MIS Executive to focus on analysis rather than manual formatting.

---

**26. What is the difference between Raw Data and Analyzed Data?**

Raw data is unprocessed data directly from a source system — it contains all records, may have duplicates, inconsistencies, and is not formatted for reading. Analyzed data is raw data that has been cleaned, aggregated, summarized, and enriched with context, comparisons, and insights. For example, a 50,000-row call log is raw data; a table showing calls by agent with AHT, FCR, and CSAT vs. target is analyzed data. MIS transforms raw data into analyzed data.

---

**27. How do you ensure data confidentiality in reports?**

Data confidentiality is maintained by: password-protecting sensitive Excel files, restricting report distribution to authorized recipients only, hiding or removing sensitive columns (like salary or personal data) before sharing, and using role-based access when publishing to shared drives or dashboards. Salary reports, attrition data, and financial P&L should never be sent on open email threads. Always follow your company's data handling and GDPR/privacy policies.

---

**28. What tools do you use for MIS reporting?**

Commonly used MIS tools include: Microsoft Excel (pivot tables, formulas, macros — primary tool), Power BI or Tableau (interactive dashboards), Google Sheets (for real-time collaboration), SQL (for data extraction from databases), and ERP systems like SAP or Tally (for financial and operational data). Many companies also use Power Automate for workflow automation. Proficiency in Excel is the baseline requirement; Power BI and SQL are increasingly expected.

---

**29. How do you communicate a sudden performance drop to a manager?**

Communicate a sudden drop by: (1) flagging it proactively — don't wait for the manager to notice, (2) providing context — when did it start, what changed, (3) sharing the root cause if known, or stating that investigation is ongoing, (4) quantifying the impact, and (5) sharing the corrective action plan with a timeline. Example: "CSAT dropped from 82% to 71% this week — preliminary analysis points to a new agent batch. Re-training is scheduled for Monday."

---

**30. What questions do you ask before building a new report?**

Before building a new report, ask: (1) Who is the audience — team lead, manager, or C-suite? (2) What decision will this report support? (3) What metrics need to be tracked and with what frequency? (4) Where does the data come from and who owns it? (5) What is the delivery format — Excel, PDF, email, dashboard? (6) What is the deadline for the first delivery? Gathering these requirements upfront prevents rework and ensures the report is genuinely useful.
