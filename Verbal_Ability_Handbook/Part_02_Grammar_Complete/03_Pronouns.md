## 3.1 Types of Pronouns — Complete Classification

### The Complete Pronoun Family

```
PRONOUNS
├── Personal Pronouns (I, you, he, she, it, we, they)
├── Possessive Pronouns (mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs)
├── Reflexive Pronouns (myself, yourself, himself...)
├── Intensive Pronouns (myself, yourself... for emphasis)
├── Demonstrative Pronouns (this, that, these, those)
├── Relative Pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that)
├── Interrogative Pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, what)
├── Indefinite Pronouns (everyone, someone, anyone, each...)
└── Reciprocal Pronouns (each other, one another)
```

---

### 3.1.1 Personal Pronouns

Personal pronouns refer to specific people or things and change form based on **person**, **number**, **gender**, and **case**.

| Person | Subjective | Objective | Possessive Adj | Possessive Pro |
|--------|-----------|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| 1st Singular | **I** | me | my | mine |
| 2nd Singular | **you** | you | your | yours |
| 3rd Singular (M) | **he** | him | his | his |
| 3rd Singular (F) | **she** | her | her | hers |
| 3rd Singular (N) | **it** | it | its | its |
| 1st Plural | **we** | us | our | ours |
| 2nd Plural | **you** | you | your | yours |
| 3rd Plural | **they** | them | their | theirs |

**Case Rules:**

```
rule
SUBJECTIVE CASE: Use when the pronoun is the SUBJECT of the verb.
OBJECTIVE CASE: Use when the pronoun is the OBJECT of a verb or preposition.
POSSESSIVE: Shows ownership.
```

**Examples:**
- **I** called him. (I = subject, him = object)
- **She** gave **them** the report. (she = subject, them = object)
- **We** saw **her** at the conference. (we = subject, her = object)
- Between **you** and **me**, this is difficult. (after preposition 'between' → objective: me, not I)

> [!WARNING]
> **Classic Error:** "Between you and **I**" → WRONG. Prepositions always take objective case.
> ✅ Correct: "Between you and **me**."
> ✅ Correct: "This is a matter between **him** and **her**."

---

### 3.1.2 Possessive Pronouns vs Possessive Adjectives

| | Possessive Adjective (before noun) | Possessive Pronoun (standalone) |
|-|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Definition | Modifies a noun | Replaces noun phrase |
| Examples | **my** book, **his** car, **their** house | The book is **mine**. The car is **his**. |
| Usage | Always before a noun | Never directly before a noun |

**Common Errors:**
- ❌ "Is this **your's**?" → ✅ "Is this **yours**?" (No apostrophe in possessive pronouns)
- ❌ "The fault is **their's**." → ✅ "The fault is **theirs**."
- ❌ "**It's** leg was broken." → ✅ "**Its** leg was broken." (its = possessive; it's = it is)

> [!TIP]
> **Memory Trick:** Possessive pronouns NEVER have apostrophes: mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs. Apostrophe = contraction (it's = it is; you're = you are; they're = they are).

## 3.2 Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement

**Definition:** A pronoun must agree with its **antecedent** (the noun it replaces) in:
1. **Number** (singular/plural)
2. **Gender** (masculine/feminine/neutral)
3. **Person** (1st/2nd/3rd)

```
rule
The pronoun MUST match its antecedent in number, gender, and person.
```

### Rule 3.2.1 — Singular Antecedents → Singular Pronouns

- Every student must submit **his or her** assignment. (singular 'every student')
- Each employee is responsible for **his or her** work.
- The company revised **its** policies. (company → its)

> [!NOTE]
> **Singular "they":** In modern English, "they" is increasingly accepted as a gender-neutral singular pronoun: "Every student should do **their** best." This is becoming standard in informal and even formal writing. In placement exams, the traditional "his or her" is still preferred.

---

### Rule 3.2.2 — Compound Antecedents with AND → Plural Pronoun

When two antecedents are joined by AND, use a **plural** pronoun:
- Ravi and Priya brought **their** laptops.
- The manager and the director submitted **their** reports.

---

### Rule 3.2.3 — Compound Antecedents with OR/NOR → Pronoun Agrees with NEAREST

```
rule
EITHER...OR / NEITHER...NOR → Pronoun agrees with the CLOSER antecedent
```

| Sentence | Correct Pronoun |
|----------|----------------|
| Either the manager or the employees will do **their** job. | their (employees = nearest) |
| Either the employees or the manager will do **his** job. | his (manager = nearest) |
| Neither John nor Mary brought **her** notes. | her (Mary = nearest) |
| Neither the girls nor the boy did **his** work. | his (boy = nearest) |

---

### Rule 3.2.4 — Collective Noun Antecedents

- Collective noun acting as UNIT → singular pronoun (it/its)
- Collective noun members acting individually → plural pronoun (they/their)

**Examples:**
- The team won **its** match. (unit action)
- The team were arguing among **themselves**. (individual action)
- The committee submitted **its** report. (unit action)

---

### Rule 3.2.5 — Indefinite Pronoun Antecedents

| Indefinite Pronoun | Number | Pronoun to Use |
|-------------------|--------|---------------|
| everyone, everybody | Singular | his/her (or their — modern) |
| someone, somebody | Singular | his/her |
| anyone, anybody | Singular | his/her |
| no one, nobody | Singular | his/her |
| each, either, neither | Singular | his/her |
| both, few, many, several | Plural | their |
| all, some, most | Depends on noun | it/their |

**Examples:**
- Everyone has **his or her** own opinion.
- Neither of the candidates accepted **his** defeat gracefully.
- Both the girls submitted **their** assignments.
- All the water lost **its** purity.
- All the students raised **their** hands.

## 3.3 WHO vs WHOM — The He/Him Trick

> [!IMPORTANT]
> WHO vs WHOM is one of the MOST TESTED pronoun distinctions in ALL placement exams. This trick will save you every time.

### The Substitution Trick

```
rule
THE HE/HIM TRICK:
If you can substitute HE/SHE/THEY → Use WHO (subjective)
If you can substitute HIM/HER/THEM → Use WHOM (objective)

WHO = HE (both end with a vowel... sort of — both are subjects)
WHOM = HIM (both end in M — objective case)
```

**Step-by-Step Method:**
1. Find the clause where who/whom is used.
2. Rearrange the clause if necessary.
3. Substitute HE or HIM.
4. If HE fits → WHO. If HIM fits → WHOM.

### Worked Examples:

**Example 1:** "___ called you?"
- Rearrange: HE called you? or HIM called you?
- HE called you → ✅ → Use **WHO**
- Answer: **Who** called you?

**Example 2:** "___ did you call?"
- Rearrange: You called HE? or You called HIM?
- You called HIM → ✅ → Use **WHOM**
- Answer: **Whom** did you call?

**Example 3:** "The manager ___ I recommended has been promoted."
- Rearrange: I recommended HE? or I recommended HIM?
- I recommended HIM → ✅ → Use **WHOM**
- Answer: The manager **whom** I recommended has been promoted.

**Example 4:** "She is the one ___ always arrives late."
- Rearrange: HE always arrives late? or HIM always arrives late?
- HE always arrives late → ✅ → Use **WHO**
- Answer: She is the one **who** always arrives late.

**Example 5:** "To ___ should I address the letter?"
- Rearrange: Should I address the letter to HIM?
- HIM → ✅ → Use **WHOM**
- Answer: To **whom** should I address the letter?

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic:** WHO-HE (both are subject forms). WHOM-HIM (both end in M, both are object forms).

### Table: WHO vs WHOM

| Use | Case | Example |
|-----|------|---------|
| Subject of verb | Subjective | **Who** is calling? |
| Complement of verb | Subjective | It is **who**? → formal: It is **he** |
| Object of verb | Objective | **Whom** did they invite? |
| Object of preposition | Objective | With **whom** did you speak? |
| Relative clause subject | Subjective | The man **who** called... |
| Relative clause object | Objective | The man **whom** we called... |

## 3.4 WHOEVER vs WHOMEVER

Same logic as WHO vs WHOM — apply the HE/HIM test:

```
rule
WHOEVER = WHO (subject case) — functions as subject
WHOMEVER = WHOM (object case) — functions as object
```

**Key Insight:** The entire clause starting with whoever/whomever functions as a noun clause. Determine the function WITHIN that clause.

**Examples:**
- "Give the prize to **whoever** wins." 
  → whoever wins = the subject of "wins" → subjective → whoever ✅
  
- "Give the prize to **whomever** you choose."
  → you choose [him] = him is the object of "choose" → objective → whomever ✅

- "**Whoever** arrives first will get the reward."
  → [He] arrives first → subject → whoever ✅

- "Hire **whomever** you think is qualified."
  → This is tricky! "you think" is a parenthetical = "you think [he] is qualified" → he → subject → whoever!
  → Actually: **whoever** you think is qualified (not whomever) ← common mistake!

> [!WARNING]
> **The "I think / you think" Trap:**
> "Give this to whoever **you think** deserves it."
> Mistake: treating "you think" as the main verb making "whoever" the object.
> Reality: "you think" is a parenthetical; the true structure is "whoever deserves it" (HE deserves it → WHO) → **whoever** ✅

## 3.5 Reflexive Pronouns — Rules and Traps

### Rule 1: Reflexive Use — When Subject and Object Are the Same Person

```
rule
Use reflexive pronouns when the SUBJECT and OBJECT refer to the SAME PERSON.
```

- She hurt **herself**. (she = herself = same person)
- He blamed **himself** for the mistake.
- They enjoyed **themselves** at the party.
- I taught **myself** to code.

---

### Rule 2: Intensive Use — For Emphasis

```
rule
Reflexive pronouns can be used INTENSIVELY to emphasize the noun/pronoun they follow.
They can be REMOVED without changing the sentence meaning.
```

- The president **himself** signed the document. (emphasis — not essential)
- I **myself** will handle this matter. (emphatic)
- She completed the project **herself**. (emphasis — she did it, no one helped)

**Test for Intensive:** Remove the reflexive pronoun. If the sentence still makes grammatical sense, it's intensive use.

---

### Rule 3: Reflexive Pronouns CANNOT Replace Subject Pronouns

```
rule
Reflexive pronouns CANNOT be the SUBJECT of a sentence or clause.
```

**Errors:**
- ❌ "**Myself** and Ravi will handle this." → ✅ "**I** and Ravi will handle this." (or better: "Ravi and **I**")
- ❌ "Please contact **myself** for more information." → ✅ "Please contact **me**."
- ❌ "The report was submitted by Priya and **myself**." → ✅ "The report was submitted by Priya and **me**."

> [!WARNING]
> The misuse of "myself" as a polite-sounding substitute for "I" or "me" is EXTREMELY common in formal Indian communication. It is always WRONG. Use I (subject) or me (object).

---

### Rule 4: Reflexive Pronouns After Prepositions (Referring Back to Subject)

- She is proud of **herself**.
- He kept the secret to **himself**.
- They blamed **themselves**.

BUT — when the preposition phrase does NOT refer back to the subject:
- ❌ "She sat between Ravi and **herself**." (incorrect — the preposition is not reflexive here)
- ✅ "She sat between Ravi and **her**." (her = different person from subject)

## 3.6 Ambiguous Pronoun Reference

**Definition:** When a pronoun can logically refer to more than one antecedent, the reference is **ambiguous**. This is a major error category in placement sentence correction questions.

```
rule
Every pronoun must have a CLEAR, UNAMBIGUOUS antecedent.
```

### Types of Ambiguous References:

#### Type 1: Two Possible Antecedents

- ❌ "Ravi told Sanjay that **he** had won the prize." (Who won — Ravi or Sanjay?)
- ✅ "Ravi told Sanjay, 'I have won the prize.'" (Clear: Ravi won)
- ✅ "Ravi told Sanjay that **Sanjay** had won the prize." (Repeat the noun)

#### Type 2: Pronoun Without a Clear Antecedent

- ❌ "When I got to the store, **they** said it was closed." (Who is 'they'?)
- ✅ "When I got to the store, **the staff** said it was closed."

#### Type 3: "It" and "This" Used Vaguely

- ❌ "The company raised salaries and reduced hours. **This** improved morale."
  (What improved morale? The salaries? The hours? Both?)
- ✅ "The company raised salaries and reduced hours. **These changes** improved morale."

#### Type 4: "Which" with Unclear Referent

- ❌ "She passed the exam after failing twice, which pleased her family."
  (What pleased them — passing the exam? Or that she failed twice?)
- ✅ "She passed the exam after failing twice, and **this success** pleased her family."

## 3.7 IT vs THEY for Collective Nouns

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This is a heavily tested concept in error detection questions.

### The Rule:

```
rule
Collective noun as a UNIT → IT/ITS (singular pronoun)
Collective noun acting as INDIVIDUALS → THEY/THEIR (plural pronoun)

For COMPANIES/ORGANIZATIONS: IT/ITS (always treated as singular)
For TEAMS acting individually: THEY/THEIR
```

| Sentence | Pronoun | Reason |
|----------|---------|--------|
| The team won the match. **It** was their 10th victory. | IT | Team as a unit |
| The committee submitted **its** report. | ITS | Acting as a unit |
| Google has changed **its** algorithm. | ITS | Company = singular |
| The jury reached **its** verdict. | ITS | Acting as a unit |
| The team were arguing; **they** couldn't agree. | THEY | Members individually |

> [!NOTE]
> **British vs American:** In British English, companies/teams are often referred to as "they" (Manchester United have announced **they** will...). In American English, "it" is standard. For Indian placement exams, use IT for companies, organizations, and teams acting as units.

## 3.8 Relative Pronouns — WHO, WHOM, WHOSE, WHICH, THAT

### The Complete Guide to Relative Pronouns

| Relative Pronoun | Refers To | Used In |
|-----------------|-----------|---------|
| **who** | People (subject) | Defining & non-defining clauses |
| **whom** | People (object) | Defining & non-defining clauses |
| **whose** | People, Animals, Things (possessive) | Both clause types |
| **which** | Things, Animals | Non-defining clauses; defining clauses |
| **that** | People, Things | Defining clauses ONLY |

---

### THAT vs WHICH — The Defining vs Non-Defining Rule

```
rule
THAT → DEFINING (restrictive) clauses — NO COMMA before it
WHICH → NON-DEFINING (non-restrictive) clauses — COMMA before it
```

| Type | Example | Meaning |
|------|---------|---------|
| Defining (THAT) | The book **that** I bought was excellent. | Identifies WHICH book |
| Non-Defining (WHICH) | The book, **which** I bought last week, was excellent. | Adds extra info |

**How to decide:**
- Can you REMOVE the clause without losing essential meaning? → Non-defining → **WHICH** (with comma)
- Is the clause NECESSARY to identify the noun? → Defining → **THAT** (no comma)

**Examples:**
- ✅ The software **that** we developed won an award. (defines which software)
- ✅ Python, **which** is a programming language, is widely used. (extra info)
- ✅ The employee **who** solved the problem was promoted. (person, subject)
- ✅ The employee **whom** we hired is very talented. (person, object)
- ✅ The company **whose** revenue doubled received the award. (possessive)

> [!WARNING]
> **THAT cannot follow a comma.** If you see a comma before the relative pronoun, use WHICH (for things) or WHO (for people), never THAT.
> - ❌ "The project, **that** took 6 months, was successful."
> - ✅ "The project, **which** took 6 months, was successful."

---

### Omitting the Relative Pronoun (Zero Relative)

In defining clauses where the relative pronoun is the OBJECT, it can be omitted:

- The man **[whom] I met** yesterday was kind. → "The man I met yesterday was kind." ✅
- The book **[that] I read** was interesting. → "The book I read was interesting." ✅
- The idea **[that] she proposed** was accepted. → "The idea she proposed was accepted." ✅

BUT: Cannot omit if relative pronoun is the SUBJECT:
- The man **who** called me → cannot become "The man called me" (changes meaning)
- The book **which** impressed me → cannot be omitted

## 3.9 Indefinite Pronouns — Agreement Rules

### Singular Indefinite Pronouns (Always Singular Verb)

```
rule
MEMORIZE: ONE, ANYONE, EVERYONE, SOMEONE, NO ONE
ANYBODY, EVERYBODY, SOMEBODY, NOBODY
EACH, EITHER, NEITHER
→ ALL TAKE SINGULAR VERBS AND SINGULAR PRONOUNS
```

| Pronoun | Correct Usage |
|---------|--------------|
| everyone | Everyone **is** here. Everyone did **his/her** best. |
| someone | Someone **has** left **his/her** bag. |
| no one | No one **was** present. |
| each | Each **is** responsible for **his/her** work. |
| either | Either **is** acceptable. |
| neither | Neither **was** selected. |

---

### Plural Indefinite Pronouns (Always Plural Verb)

```
rule
BOTH, FEW, MANY, SEVERAL → PLURAL verb and pronoun
```

- **Both** are qualified candidates.
- **Many** have submitted **their** forms.
- **Few** realized **their** mistake.

---

### Variable Indefinite Pronouns (Depends on Context)

```
rule
SOME, ANY, NONE, ALL, MOST → singular or plural depending on the noun they refer to
```

| Example | Verb | Reason |
|---------|------|--------|
| Some of the water **is** gone. | Singular | Water = uncountable |
| Some of the students **are** present. | Plural | Students = countable plural |
| All of the report **was** submitted. | Singular | Report = singular |
| All of the reports **were** submitted. | Plural | Reports = plural |
| None of the money **was** found. | Singular | Money = uncountable |
| None of the students **were** present. | Plural | Students = countable plural |

> [!NOTE]
> **NONE — Singular or Plural?** This is one of the most debated grammar points. Traditionally, NONE = not one = singular. But modern grammar accepts both:
> - "None of them **is** willing." (formal/strict)
> - "None of them **are** willing." (modern/accepted)
> In placement exams: if NONE is followed by an uncountable or singular noun → singular; if followed by plural countable → plural is commonly accepted.

## 3.10 Reciprocal Pronouns — EACH OTHER vs ONE ANOTHER

```
rule
EACH OTHER → used for exactly TWO people/things
ONE ANOTHER → used for THREE or more people/things
```

| Example | Correct? | Reason |
|---------|----------|--------|
| The two friends helped **each other**. | ✅ | Two people |
| The three colleagues helped **one another**. | ✅ | Three or more |
| The two girls respect **one another**. | ❌ (technically) | Should be each other |
| The team members supported **each other**. | Acceptable informally | Team = many |

> [!NOTE]
> In modern English, this distinction is blurring and both are often used interchangeably. But in placement exam error detection, follow the strict rule.

## 3.11 Practice Questions (25 Questions)

---

**Q1. The manager, along with his colleagues, submitted ___ report.**

(A) their
(B) its
(C) his
(D) our

**✅ Answer: (C) his**

**📖 Explanation:** "The manager" is the subject. "Along with his colleagues" is a parenthetical phrase (not part of the grammatical subject). The pronoun refers back to "the manager" (singular, male) → "his."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "their" is plural — manager is singular.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "its" is used for things/companies, not a person.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "our" is 1st person plural — wrong reference.

**📏 Rule:** Parenthetical phrases (along with, together with, as well as) don't change the number of the subject.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Subject = manager (singular, male) → his.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys

---

**Q2. ___ did you speak with about the job offer?**

(A) Who
(B) Whom
(C) Whoever
(D) Whomever

**✅ Answer: (B) Whom**

**📖 Explanation:** Apply the HE/HIM test: "You spoke with HIM about the job offer." → HIM → WHOM. The pronoun is the object of the preposition "with."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO is subjective case — here the pronoun is the object.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** Whoever is for indefinite/subordinate clauses.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** Whomever is for indefinite/subordinate clauses.

**📏 Rule:** Preposition + whom (objective case). WHO = subject, WHOM = object.
**⚡ Shortcut:** HIM → WHOM. Both end in M (or have "m"). Both = object.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Accenture

---

**Q3. Please send the invitation to ___ is interested in attending.**

(A) whoever
(B) whomever
(C) who
(D) whom

**✅ Answer: (A) whoever**

**📖 Explanation:** "...to whoever is interested" — the clause "whoever is interested" is a noun clause acting as object of "to." Within the clause, [he] is interested → HE → WHO form → WHOEVER (not whomever).

**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "Whomever is interested" — whomever would need to be the object within its clause. But here, "whoever" is the SUBJECT of "is interested."
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** WHO is correct form, but WHOEVER is needed for the indefinite noun clause.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHOM would be object; here it's the subject.

**📏 Rule:** WHOEVER = subject within its clause. WHOMEVER = object within its clause.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Inside the clause → substitute HE or HIM → HE → whoever; HIM → whomever.
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** Infosys, IBM

---

**Q4. Each of the students must submit ___ assignment by Friday.**

(A) their
(B) its
(C) his or her
(D) our

**✅ Answer: (C) his or her**

**📖 Explanation:** "Each" is a singular indefinite pronoun. "Students" is plural, but the verb and pronoun agree with "each" (singular). Traditional grammar: "his or her." Modern: "their" is also acceptable, but placement exams prefer the formal "his or her."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "their" is plural — "each" is singular (though informally acceptable).
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "its" is for non-human things.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "our" is 1st person plural.

**📏 Rule:** EACH → singular → "his or her" in formal writing.
**⚡ Shortcut:** EACH = ONE person = singular = his or her.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Wipro

---

**Q5. The company has revised ___ hiring policy for the upcoming fiscal year.**

(A) their
(B) its
(C) his
(D) our

**✅ Answer: (B) its**

**📖 Explanation:** Companies and organizations are treated as singular entities → "it/its." "The company has revised **its** hiring policy."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "their" is plural — company is singular (entity).
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "his" is for individual males.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "our" is 1st person plural.

**📏 Rule:** Companies/Organizations = singular → IT/ITS.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Company → its (not their). Person → his/her. Multiple people → their.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** All companies (very common)

---

**Q6. The committee has made ___ final decision regarding the project.**

(A) their
(B) its
(C) his
(D) our

**✅ Answer: (B) its**

**📖 Explanation:** "Committee has made" — singular verb (acting as a unit) → singular pronoun "its."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "their" would go with "have made" (plural). Since committee is acting as a unit here, singular pronoun is needed.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "his" is for a specific male person.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "our" is 1st person.

**📏 Rule:** Collective noun (unit action) → its.
**⚡ Shortcut:** If it's a "has/is/was" verb (singular) → its.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** IBM, TCS

---

**Q7. Ravi told Priya that ___ would be late for the meeting.**

(A) he
(B) she
(C) they
(D) Cannot determine from context

**✅ Answer: (D) Cannot determine from context**

**📖 Explanation:** "Ravi told Priya that ___ would be late" — the pronoun could refer to Ravi (he) or Priya (she). This is a classic **ambiguous pronoun reference** error. Without more context, we cannot determine who would be late.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** Could be correct if Ravi is late, but we can't confirm.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** Could be correct if Priya is late, but we can't confirm.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "They" would refer to both of them or a third party.

**📏 Rule:** Ambiguous pronoun reference — the pronoun must clearly refer to ONE antecedent.
**⚡ Shortcut:** When a sentence has two possible antecedents for a pronoun → it's an error/ambiguity.
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Accenture (error detection)

**Q8. Neither the director nor the employees received ___ bonus.**

(A) their
(B) its
(C) his
(D) her

**✅ Answer: (A) their**

**📖 Explanation:** "Neither...nor..." — pronoun agrees with the NEAREST antecedent: "employees" (plural) → their. Wait — actually: In "neither...nor" constructions, the verb AND pronoun agree with the closer element. "employees" is closest → plural → "their."

**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "its" is for organizations, not people.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "his" would only be correct if the nearest antecedent were a singular male noun.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "her" would only apply to a singular female.

**📏 Rule:** NEITHER...NOR / EITHER...OR → pronoun agrees with the NEARER antecedent.
**⚡ Shortcut:** In X or/nor Y → pronoun agrees with Y (the nearer one).
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** Infosys, TCS NQT

---

**Q9. The employee ___ you recommended for the position has resigned.**

(A) who
(B) whom
(C) whose
(D) which

**✅ Answer: (B) whom**

**📖 Explanation:** HIM/HE test: "You recommended HIM for the position." → HIM → WHOM. The relative pronoun is the object of "recommended."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO is subject form — here it's the object.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** WHOSE shows possession — no possession indicated here.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHICH refers to things, not people.

**📏 Rule:** WHO = subject, WHOM = object. Use HE/HIM test.
**⚡ Shortcut:** "You recommended HIM" → HIM → WHOM.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Accenture, Infosys

---

**Q10. She hurt ___ while trying to fix the machine.**

(A) her
(B) herself
(C) hers
(D) she

**✅ Answer: (B) herself**

**📖 Explanation:** Subject (she) = Object (herself) → same person → reflexive pronoun. "She hurt herself."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "her" would imply she hurt another female person.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "hers" is a possessive pronoun — wrong function.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "she" is subjective — cannot be the object.

**📏 Rule:** Same subject and object → reflexive pronoun.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Subject and object = same person → add -self/-selves.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** All companies

---

**Q11. Please send any queries to Priya or ___.**

(A) I
(B) myself
(C) me
(D) mine

**✅ Answer: (C) me**

**📖 Explanation:** "Send to Priya or ___" — "to" is a preposition. After prepositions, use the OBJECTIVE case: "me" (not "I"). Also, "myself" cannot replace "me" here because there's no reflexive meaning.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "I" is subjective — used as subject, not after prepositions.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "Myself" cannot be used as a simple object pronoun. No reflexive/intensive meaning here.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "Mine" is a possessive pronoun — wrong function.

**📏 Rule:** Preposition → objective case (me, him, her, us, them). Never "I" or "myself" after prepositions.
**⚡ Shortcut:** "To/for/with/between" → always me, him, her — NEVER I, myself.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, IBM

---

**Q12. The project, ___ took six months to complete, was a great success.**

(A) that
(B) who
(C) which
(D) whose

**✅ Answer: (C) which**

**📖 Explanation:** The relative clause ", ___ took six months" is non-defining (note the comma before and after). Non-defining clauses use WHICH (for things), not THAT. The project is a thing → WHICH.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** THAT is used in DEFINING clauses (no comma). Cannot follow a comma.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** WHO is for people, not projects.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHOSE shows possession — the project doesn't possess anything here.

**📏 Rule:** Comma + relative clause = non-defining → WHICH (things) or WHO (people); never THAT.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Comma before relative pronoun → WHICH or WHO (never THAT).
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Wipro

**Q13. Between ___ and ___, this matter must be resolved today.**

(A) I, she
(B) me, her
(C) I, her
(D) me, she

**✅ Answer: (B) me, her**

**📖 Explanation:** "Between" is a preposition. All pronouns after a preposition must be in the OBJECTIVE case. Me (objective of I) and her (objective of she).

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Between I and she" — both pronouns are in subjective case, which is wrong after a preposition.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "I" is subjective — wrong after preposition.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "she" is subjective — wrong after preposition.

**📏 Rule:** Preposition → objective case for ALL pronouns following it.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Between → ALWAYS me, him, her, us, them. NEVER I, he, she, we, they.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** Accenture, TCS

---

**Q14. ___ book is this — yours or his?**

(A) Who's
(B) Whose
(C) Who
(D) Whom

**✅ Answer: (B) Whose**

**📖 Explanation:** WHOSE is the possessive relative/interrogative pronoun asking about possession. "Whose book?" = "Who does this book belong to?"

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Who's" = "Who is" (contraction). "Who is book is this?" — wrong.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Who book" — WHO is not a possessive form.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHOM is objective — not possessive.

**📏 Rule:** WHOSE = possessive; WHO'S = who + is (contraction).
**⚡ Shortcut:** Whose/His/Her/Its = no apostrophe for possessives. It's/Who's = contractions.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Wipro

**Q15. The police ___ investigating the matter and ___ have made an arrest.**

(A) is, they
(B) are, they
(C) is, it
(D) are, it

**✅ Answer: (B) are, they**

**📖 Explanation:** "Police" is always plural (special collective noun that behaves as plural). → "are investigating." The pronoun "they" refers back to police (plural).

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Police is" — police is always plural.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Police is" is wrong; "it" for police is also wrong.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "they" is correct but (B) has "are" which is also needed.

**📏 Rule:** POLICE is always plural → plural verb AND plural pronoun.
**⚡ Shortcut:** POLICE = always PLURAL. Special exception among collective nouns.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Infosys, IBM

**Q16. Everybody has ___ right to express ___ opinion freely.**

(A) their, their
(B) his or her, his or her
(C) its, its
(D) our, our

**✅ Answer: (B) his or her, his or her**

**📖 Explanation:** "Everybody" is singular → singular possessive pronoun "his or her." Both blanks refer back to "everybody."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "their" is plural — "everybody" is singular in formal grammar.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "its" is for non-human things.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "our" is 1st person plural.

**📏 Rule:** Everybody/everyone/somebody/anyone → singular → his or her.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Every-body = one body = singular = his or her.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Accenture

**Q17. The two friends supported ___ throughout the difficult journey.**

(A) one another
(B) each other
(C) themselves
(D) them

**✅ Answer: (B) each other**

**📖 Explanation:** "The two friends" — exactly two people → use "each other" (reciprocal pronoun for two). "One another" is for three or more.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "One another" is for three or more people.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Themselves" = reflexive (they supported themselves = each person supported themselves individually, not a mutual support sense).
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "Them" would mean they supported some other people.

**📏 Rule:** Two people → each other. Three or more → one another.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Two = each other (2 words, 2 people). One another = one + another = more than 2.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Wipro, Cognizant

**Q18. It was ___ who solved the problem first.**

(A) her
(B) she
(C) hers
(D) herself

**✅ Answer: (B) she**

**📖 Explanation:** "It was ___ who solved the problem." In formal grammar, when a pronoun follows "it is/was," it takes the SUBJECTIVE case: "It was **she** who solved it." This is because the pronoun is the complement of "it was" and is equivalent to the subject.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "her" is objective — technically wrong in formal grammar (though colloquially common: "It was her who did it").
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "hers" is possessive pronoun.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "herself" is reflexive — no reflexive meaning here.

**📏 Rule:** "It is/was + pronoun + who/that" → subjective case (he, she, I, we, they).
**⚡ Shortcut:** It was HE, SHE, I, WE, THEY (subjects after "it was"). Not him, her, me, us, them.
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Infosys

---

**Q19. Neither of them ___ responsible for ___ actions.**

(A) are, their
(B) is, their
(C) is, his
(D) are, his

**✅ Answer: (B) is, their**

**📖 Explanation:** "Neither of them" → NEITHER is singular → singular verb "is." But the pronoun referring back to "them" (which represents multiple people) → "their" actions. This is one of those mixed cases where the verb is singular (because of "neither") but the pronoun refers to "them" (plural antecedent) → "their."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Neither...are" — NEITHER takes singular verb.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "his" would only work if both were male and we're treating the nearest/single antecedent, but "them" implies plural subjects, so "their" is more natural.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "are" with "neither" is wrong.

**📏 Rule:** NEITHER (as pronoun) = singular verb. But the pronoun can refer back to the plural "them" = their.
**⚡ Shortcut:** NEITHER = is/was. The pronoun for the PEOPLE involved = their (if mixed/plural group).
**📊 Difficulty:** Expert
**🏢 Company:** IBM, TCS NQT

**Q20. The report ___ was submitted by our team won the best analysis award.**

(A) who
(B) whom
(C) which
(D) that

**✅ Answer: (D) that**

**📖 Explanation:** "The report ___ was submitted by our team" — this is a DEFINING clause (it tells us which report — the one our team submitted). For defining clauses referring to things: THAT. No comma before the clause → defining → THAT.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO is for people.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** WHOM is for people (objective).
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** WHICH could be used in a defining clause, but without a comma, THAT is preferred. (In placement exams, THAT is the standard answer for defining clauses about things.)

**📏 Rule:** THAT for defining clauses (no comma) about things; WHICH for non-defining (with comma).
**⚡ Shortcut:** No comma + thing → THAT. Comma + thing → WHICH.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Accenture, Wipro

**Q21. ___ submitted the project first will be awarded extra credit.**

(A) Who
(B) Whoever
(C) Whom
(D) Whomever

**✅ Answer: (B) Whoever**

**📖 Explanation:** "Whoever submitted the project first" — this is a noun clause acting as the subject of "will be awarded." Within the clause: [he] submitted → HE → WHO form → WHOEVER (indefinite).

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO begins a question or identifies a specific relative clause; WHOEVER is needed for an indefinite/unknown subject.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** WHOM is objective — here, the pronoun is the subject of "submitted."
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHOMEVER is for when the pronoun is the object within its clause.

**📏 Rule:** WHOEVER = indefinite subject pronoun (anyone who).
**⚡ Shortcut:** HE submitted → HE → WHO → WHOEVER (indefinite context).
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Infosys

**Q22. The CEO ___ company won the award attended the ceremony.**

(A) who
(B) which
(C) whose
(D) whom

**✅ Answer: (C) whose**

**📖 Explanation:** "The CEO ___ company won the award" → the company BELONGS to the CEO → possession → WHOSE. "The CEO whose company won the award attended the ceremony."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO is used as subject — here possession is needed.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** WHICH refers to things, and this refers to the CEO's company.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHOM is objective — not possessive.

**📏 Rule:** WHOSE = possessive relative pronoun for people and things.
**⚡ Shortcut:** ___ + noun (showing possession) → WHOSE. "Whose company" = CEO's company.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, All companies

**Q23. I myself will handle the presentation.**

The word "myself" in this sentence is used as:

(A) Reflexive pronoun
(B) Intensive pronoun
(C) Object pronoun
(D) Possessive pronoun

**✅ Answer: (B) Intensive pronoun**

**📖 Explanation:** "I **myself** will handle the presentation" — 'myself' is used for EMPHASIS. It emphasizes that *I* specifically will do it. You can remove "myself" and the sentence still makes perfect sense: "I will handle the presentation." → That's the test for intensive use.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** Reflexive use = subject and object are the same person. Here, myself is not the object.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Myself" is not functioning as an object here.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** Possessive pronoun would be "mine."

**📏 Rule:** Intensive pronoun = adds emphasis; can be removed without changing sentence meaning.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Remove it — sentence still works? → Intensive. Remove it — sentence breaks? → Reflexive.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Wipro, Cognizant

---

**Q24. All of the water ___ evaporated due to the heat.**

(A) have
(B) were
(C) has
(D) are

**✅ Answer: (C) has**

**📖 Explanation:** "All of the water" — ALL is a variable pronoun; when it refers to an UNCOUNTABLE noun (water), it takes a SINGULAR verb: "has evaporated."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "have" is plural — but water is uncountable → singular verb.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "were" is plural past.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "are" is plural present.

**📏 Rule:** All/Some/Most/None + uncountable noun → singular verb.
**⚡ Shortcut:** ALL + water/money/information (uncountable) → HAS/IS/WAS. ALL + people/books (plural countable) → HAVE/ARE/WERE.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Infosys, IBM

**Q25. She is the sort of person ___ you can rely on completely.**

(A) who
(B) whom
(C) whose
(D) which

**✅ Answer: (B) whom**

**📖 Explanation:** HIM/HE test: "You can rely on HIM/HER completely." → HIM → WHOM. "Rely on" takes an object → objective case → WHOM. Also, "whom" can be thought of as the object of the preposition "on."

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** WHO is subjective — here the pronoun is the object of "rely on."
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** WHOSE is possessive — no possession here.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** WHICH is for things, not people.

**📏 Rule:** Object of preposition → WHOM.
**⚡ Shortcut:** "Rely on ___" → object of 'on' → HIM → WHOM.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Accenture

## 📊 Key Takeaways — Chapter 3: Pronouns

| Rule | Key Point | Common Error | Memory Aid |
|------|-----------|-------------|------------|
| WHO vs WHOM | HE→WHO; HIM→WHOM | "Whom is calling?" ❌ | WHO-HE, WHOM-HIM (both end in M) |
| Reflexive as Subject | NEVER use myself as subject | "Myself will handle it" ❌ | I/me, not myself — unless same person |
| Possessive Pronouns | No apostrophe | "their's, your's" ❌ | its, yours, theirs — no ' |
| Collective Nouns | Unit=it; Individual=they | "company revised their" ❌ | Company/committee → ITS |
| EACH/EVERY + pronoun | Singular (his or her) | "each have their" ❌ | Each = one = his/her |
| Preposition + pronoun | Objective case | "between you and I" ❌ | To/with/between → me, him, her |
| THAT vs WHICH | THAT=defining; WHICH=non-defining | "The project, that..." ❌ | Comma → WHICH. No comma → THAT |
| EACH OTHER vs ONE ANOTHER | Two=each other; 3+=one another | "two friends supported one another" ❌ | Two=2 words=each other |
