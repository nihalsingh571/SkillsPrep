## 11.1 What Are Conjunctions?

A **conjunction** is a word that **joins** words, phrases, clauses, or sentences together.

```
TYPES OF CONJUNCTIONS:
1. Coordinating   — Join EQUAL elements (clauses, words, phrases)
2. Subordinating  — Join a DEPENDENT clause to an INDEPENDENT clause
3. Correlative    — Work in PAIRS to join equal elements
4. Conjunctive Adverbs — Connect two INDEPENDENT clauses (with punctuation)
```

**Why Conjunctions Matter:**
Without conjunctions, English would be choppy and disconnected. Conjunctions signal:
- **Addition** (and, moreover, furthermore)
- **Contrast** (but, however, although, whereas)
- **Cause/Effect** (because, therefore, consequently, since)
- **Condition** (if, unless, provided that)
- **Time** (when, while, before, after, until)
- **Purpose** (so that, in order that)

## 11.2 Coordinating Conjunctions — FANBOYS

The 7 coordinating conjunctions can be remembered with the acronym **FANBOYS**:

```
F — For
A — And
N — Nor
B — But
O — Or
Y — Yet
S — So
```

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic: FANBOYS** — "**F**or **A** **N**ew **B**ook, **O**rder **Y**our **S**elf"
> This is one of the most famous grammar mnemonics in English teaching.

### Rules for FANBOYS

**Rule 1: FANBOYS join grammatically EQUAL elements**

```
Words:    He is tall AND strong.
Phrases:  She works in the morning AND in the evening.
Clauses:  I studied hard, BUT I failed the exam.
```

**Rule 2: When joining two INDEPENDENT clauses, use a COMMA before FANBOYS**

```
Correct:  She studied hard, so she passed.
Correct:  He was tired, but he continued working.
Wrong:    She studied hard so she passed. (missing comma)
```

**Rule 3: Do NOT use a comma when joining two words or phrases**

```
Wrong:    She is smart, and hardworking.
Correct:  She is smart and hardworking.
```

### Each FANBOY Explained

#### FOR — Reason/Cause

- FOR as a conjunction means "because" (formal/literary)
- It explains a reason for something
- **Always** comes between two independent clauses (never begins a sentence in formal writing)

```
Examples:
He left early, for he had a flight to catch.
She was happy, for her project was approved.
They rested, for the journey had been long.
```

> [!WARNING]
> FOR as a conjunction is easily confused with FOR as a preposition. "For" as a conjunction is followed by a complete clause, while "for" as a preposition is followed by a noun.
> - Conjunction: "He rested, **for** he was tired." (clause follows)
> - Preposition: "He rested **for** an hour." (noun follows)

#### AND — Addition

- AND adds one idea to another
- Signals addition, continuation, or simultaneous action

```
Examples:
She is intelligent and hardworking.
He ate dinner and watched TV.
The company grew, and its profits soared.
We need to analyze the data and present our findings.
```

#### NOR — Negative Addition

- NOR is used after a negative statement to add another negative
- It is always part of the "neither...nor" or "not...nor" construction
- When NOR joins two independent clauses, **inversion** (auxiliary before subject) occurs

```
Structure: [Negative clause], nor + [auxiliary] + [subject]
Examples:
She did not attend the meeting, nor did she send a representative.
He cannot write code, nor can he design interfaces.
```

> [!WARNING]
> After NOR, invert subject and auxiliary:
> ✓ "He cannot sing, **nor can he dance**." (inverted)
> ✗ "He cannot sing, **nor he can dance**." (wrong order)

#### BUT — Contrast

- BUT introduces contrast, opposition, or exception

```
Examples:
I studied hard, but I didn't pass.
The food was expensive but delicious.
She is quiet but confident.
He failed the first attempt, but he persevered.
```

#### OR — Alternative/Choice

- OR presents alternatives or choices

```
Examples:
Would you like tea or coffee?
You can email me or call me.
Hurry up, or you'll miss the train.
Is the file saved locally or on the cloud?
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **OR in a Warning/Imperative:** "Do X, or [consequence]" — the comma is important here.
> "Submit the report on time, **or** the project will be cancelled." ✓

#### YET — Contrast (Similar to BUT, more formal)

- YET introduces a surprising or unexpected contrast
- More emphatic than BUT

```
Examples:
The task was difficult, yet she completed it.
He is young, yet very experienced.
The weather was cold, yet they went hiking.
The plan seemed impossible, yet they succeeded.
```

#### SO — Result/Consequence

- SO indicates a result or consequence

```
Examples:
She worked hard, so she got promoted.
It was raining, so we cancelled the trip.
He was late, so he missed the first presentation.
The system crashed, so we lost all the data.
```

### FANBOYS Summary Table

| Conjunction | Function | Comma Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| FOR | Reason (= because) | Always comma before | He left, for he was ill. |
| AND | Addition | Comma before when joining clauses | She sang, and he danced. |
| NOR | Negative addition | Always comma + inversion | He can't sing, nor can she. |
| BUT | Contrast | Always comma before | She tried, but failed. |
| OR | Alternative | Comma before when joining clauses | Go now, or it's too late. |
| YET | Surprising contrast | Always comma before | Old, yet energetic. |
| SO | Result/Effect | Always comma before | It rained, so we stayed. |

## 11.3 Subordinating Conjunctions — The Big List

Subordinating conjunctions introduce **dependent (subordinate) clauses** and connect them to main (independent) clauses. The dependent clause CANNOT stand alone as a complete sentence.

```
Structure Option 1: [Main Clause] + [Subordinating Conjunction + Dependent Clause]
Structure Option 2: [Subordinating Conjunction + Dependent Clause], [Main Clause]
                    ↑ When dependent clause comes FIRST, use a COMMA
```

### Group 1: CAUSE / REASON

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **because** | states reason directly | She passed **because** she studied. |
| **since** | as / given that | **Since** you're here, let's begin. |
| **as** | because / while | **As** it was raining, we stayed inside. |
| **now that** | since the fact is now true | **Now that** you've joined, meet the team. |
| **inasmuch as** | to the extent that (formal) | He is responsible **inasmuch as** he signed. |
| **seeing that** | considering that | **Seeing that** we're all present, let's vote. |

> [!WARNING]
> **BECAUSE vs SINCE vs AS:**
> - **Because** = strongest, most direct reason. Use when the reason is the main point.
> - **Since** = reason is already known/assumed. "Since you're tired, sleep."
> - **As** = weakest reason word. Use for simultaneous events or light reasons.

### Group 2: CONTRAST / CONCESSION

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **although** | despite the fact that | **Although** she was ill, she worked. |
| **though** | despite (informal of although) | **Though** he tried, he failed. |
| **even though** | strong emphasis on contrast | He came **even though** it was raining. |
| **whereas** | on the other hand (contrast) | She likes tea, **whereas** he likes coffee. |
| **while** | at the same time / contrast | **While** I appreciate your help, I disagree. |
| **even if** | regardless of condition | I'll finish **even if** it takes all night. |
| **however much** | no matter how much | **However much** I try, I can't solve it. |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **ALTHOUGH vs THOUGH vs EVEN THOUGH:**
> - All three mean the same thing broadly, but...
> - **EVEN THOUGH** is the strongest (greatest surprise/contrast)
> - **ALTHOUGH** is formal and standard
> - **THOUGH** is informal; can also be used at end of sentence: "She came, **though**."

### Group 3: CONDITION

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **if** | general condition | **If** you study, you will pass. |
| **unless** | if not | You'll fail **unless** you study. |
| **provided that** | only if / on condition that | I'll help, **provided that** you try first. |
| **as long as** | only if / during the time | You can stay **as long as** you work. |
| **in case** | as a precaution | Take an umbrella **in case** it rains. |
| **on condition that** | only if | He agreed **on condition that** he'd be paid. |
| **supposing** | imagining that | **Supposing** you won the lottery, what then? |
| **assuming that** | if we assume | **Assuming that** it's true, what's the plan? |

> [!WARNING]
> **UNLESS = IF NOT:**
> - "Unless you study, you will fail." = "If you do NOT study, you will fail."
> - NEVER use UNLESS with a negative verb: "Unless you don't study" is WRONG because "unless" already means "if not" — double negative.

### Group 4: TIME

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **when** | at the time that | **When** she arrived, the meeting began. |
| **while** | during the time that | **While** he slept, she worked. |
| **before** | earlier than | Finish your work **before** you leave. |
| **after** | later than | **After** the exam, we celebrated. |
| **until / till** | up to the time that | Wait **until** I arrive. |
| **since** | from the time that | She has worked here **since** 2019. |
| **as** | at the same time as | **As** he entered, everyone stood up. |
| **as soon as** | immediately when | Call me **as soon as** you reach home. |
| **once** | after / as soon as | **Once** you understand the rule, it's easy. |
| **whenever** | every time that | **Whenever** I see her, she is smiling. |
| **by the time** | before a particular time | **By the time** he arrived, she had left. |
| **the moment (that)** | immediately when | The moment the alarm rang, she woke up. |

### Group 5: PURPOSE

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **so that** | with the intention that | She studied hard **so that** she would pass. |
| **in order that** | for the purpose that (formal) | He saved money **in order that** he could invest. |
| **lest** | for fear that (formal/literary) | She whispered **lest** she wake the baby. |

### Group 6: RESULT / CONSEQUENCE

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **so...that** | to such an extent that | He was **so** tired **that** he fell asleep. |
| **such...that** | of such a degree that | It was **such** a long journey **that** we got lost. |

### Group 7: MANNER / COMPARISON

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **as** | in the way that | Do it **as** I showed you. |
| **as if** | in the way it would be if | He spoke **as if** he knew everything. |
| **as though** | in a way suggesting | She looked **as though** she'd seen a ghost. |
| **just as** | in exactly the same way | **Just as** she left, the phone rang. |

### Group 8: PLACE

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **where** | in the place that | Park the car **where** you see the sign. |
| **wherever** | in any place that | **Wherever** you go, I will follow. |

## 11.4 Correlative Conjunctions — The Power Pairs

Correlative conjunctions work **in pairs** to join equal grammatical elements. Both parts MUST be followed by the SAME grammatical structure (parallelism).

```
KEY RULE: Both parts of a correlative pair must be followed by
          PARALLEL (same grammatical form) structures.
```

### The 6 Major Correlative Pairs

#### 1. BOTH ... AND

- Joins two positive elements
- Emphasizes that BOTH are true

```
Structure: BOTH [X] AND [X] — X must be the same form
Examples:
She is both intelligent and hardworking. (adjective + adjective) ✓
He both sings and dances. (verb + verb) ✓
Both the manager and the team leader attended. (noun + noun) ✓

WRONG: She is both intelligent and she works hard. (adj + clause) ✗
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **BOTH ... AND always takes a PLURAL verb** when the subjects are joined:
> "Both the manager **and** the team leader **were** present." (plural verb)

#### 2. EITHER ... OR

- Presents two alternatives (one or the other)
- Verb agrees with the NEARER subject (proximity rule)

```
Structure: EITHER [X] OR [X] — X must be the same form
Examples:
Either you study, or you fail. (clause + clause) ✓
She will either email or call. (verb + verb) ✓
Either the CEO or the directors will attend. → will attend (directors closer) ✓

SVA RULE: Either he or his colleagues ARE responsible. (colleagues is closer) ✓
Either his colleagues or he IS responsible. (he is closer) ✓
```

#### 3. NEITHER ... NOR

- Joins two negative alternatives (neither one nor the other)
- Verb agrees with the NEARER subject (same as either/or)
- Grammatically REPLACES "not...and not"

```
Structure: NEITHER [X] NOR [X] — X must be the same form
Examples:
Neither he nor she was present. ✓ (she is closer, singular verb)
Neither the files nor the backup WAS recovered. ✓ (backup is closer)
She is neither brave nor intelligent. (adjective + adjective) ✓
He neither smiled nor spoke. (verb + verb) ✓

WRONG: Neither he nor she were present. ✗ (verb should be was — she is closer)
```

> [!WARNING]
> Common placement trap: "Neither of the students **was** present." (Neither of + plural noun → singular verb)
> vs "Neither the students **nor** the teacher **was** present." (nor → proximity rule)

#### 4. NOT ONLY ... BUT ALSO

- Emphasizes that BOTH elements are true (adds surprise/emphasis)
- Also written as NOT ONLY ... BUT ... AS WELL

```
Structure: NOT ONLY [X] BUT ALSO [X] — X must be the same form
Examples:
She is not only talented but also dedicated. (adj + adj) ✓
He not only sings but also plays the guitar. (verb + verb) ✓
Not only was she present, but she also presented the report. (clause + clause) ✓

WRONG: She not only is talented but also she works hard. ✗ (adj + clause mismatch)
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Inversion Rule:** When "NOT ONLY" begins the sentence, the auxiliary verb must be inverted:
> "**Not only did** she win the prize, **but** she **also** received a scholarship." ✓
> "**Not only was** he wrong, **but** he **also** refused to apologize." ✓

#### 5. WHETHER ... OR

- Presents two alternatives in indirect questions or when expressing uncertainty

```
Structure: WHETHER [X] OR [X]
Examples:
I don't know whether she will come or not.
Whether you agree or disagree, this is the plan.
The question is whether to invest or to save.
```

> [!WARNING]
> WHETHER vs IF:
> - Use WHETHER (not IF) when you have two alternatives: "I don't know WHETHER she will come or not."
> - Use IF for a simple condition: "Call me IF you need help."
> - Use WHETHER (not IF) after prepositions: "We debated ABOUT whether to go." ✓

#### 6. AS ... AS

- Compares two equal elements
- The second AS is followed by a subject pronoun or noun

```
Structure: AS [adjective/adverb] AS [subject/noun]
Examples:
She is as intelligent as he (is). ✓
He runs as fast as she (does). ✓
This phone is as good as that one. ✓

The second element after AS must be NOMINATIVE (subject pronoun):
"She is as tall as I (am)." ✓ — not "as tall as me" in formal English
"He is as smart as she (is)." ✓ — not "as smart as her" in formal English
```

### Correlative Conjunctions Summary Table

| Pair | Function | SVA Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| both...and | Adds two positives | Plural verb | Both X and Y ARE present. |
| either...or | Two alternatives | Proximity rule | Either X or Y IS present. |
| neither...nor | Two negatives | Proximity rule | Neither X nor Y IS present. |
| not only...but also | Emphasis on both | Proximity rule (if two subjects) | Not only X but also Y IS here. |
| whether...or | Uncertainty/alternatives | Normal agreement | Whether X or Y matters. |
| as...as | Equal comparison | Normal agreement | As tall as I (am). |

## 11.5 Conjunctive Adverbs

Conjunctive adverbs (also called **transitional adverbs**) connect two **independent clauses**. Unlike conjunctions, they are NOT conjunctions — they are adverbs that function as transitions.

```
PUNCTUATION RULE:
[Independent Clause 1]; [Conjunctive Adverb], [Independent Clause 2]
OR
[Independent Clause 1]. [Conjunctive Adverb], [Independent Clause 2].
```

> [!WARNING]
> Using a conjunctive adverb with only a comma (no semicolon/period) creates a **comma splice** — a serious grammatical error.
> WRONG: "She studied hard, however, she failed." (comma splice)
> RIGHT: "She studied hard; however, she failed." ✓

### Complete Conjunctive Adverb List by Function

#### ADDITION

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| furthermore | also, in addition | The report is clear; furthermore, it is comprehensive. |
| moreover | additionally (stronger than furthermore) | She is talented; moreover, she is experienced. |
| also | additionally | He is a leader; he is also a mentor. |
| besides | in addition | I don't want to go; besides, it's raining. |
| in addition | also | She speaks French; in addition, she speaks Spanish. |
| likewise | in the same way | He completed his task; likewise, she finished hers. |

#### CONTRAST

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| however | in contrast, but | She tried; however, she could not succeed. |
| nevertheless | despite that, still | It was risky; nevertheless, he proceeded. |
| nonetheless | despite that (formal) | The plan failed; nonetheless, we learned. |
| on the other hand | in contrast | Python is easy; on the other hand, C++ is faster. |
| conversely | in the opposite way | Success brings joy; conversely, failure teaches. |
| instead | as an alternative | Don't panic; instead, analyze the situation. |
| still | despite this | It was late; still, she continued working. |
| yet | however | He worked hard; yet, he was not promoted. |

#### CAUSE AND EFFECT

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| therefore | as a result | She studied; therefore, she passed. |
| consequently | as a result (formal) | He broke the rule; consequently, he was fired. |
| thus | as a result (formal) | The deadline passed; thus, the project was cancelled. |
| hence | for this reason (formal) | He is experienced; hence, he leads the team. |
| as a result | because of this | The server crashed; as a result, all data was lost. |
| accordingly | in a way that matches | The plan changed; accordingly, the budget was revised. |
| for this reason | because of this | She excels at coding; for this reason, she was hired. |

#### TIME SEQUENCE

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| then | after that | Analyze the data; then, write the report. |
| next | following this | Complete the setup; next, run the test. |
| finally | at the end | Debug the code; finally, deploy the application. |
| subsequently | afterwards (formal) | He studied law; subsequently, he became a judge. |
| previously | before this | She worked at Google; previously, she was at Microsoft. |
| meanwhile | at the same time | The team coded; meanwhile, the designer created mockups. |

#### EXAMPLE / ILLUSTRATION

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| for example | to illustrate | Use AI tools; for example, ChatGPT can help with writing. |
| for instance | to illustrate | Tech skills are valued; for instance, Python is in demand. |
| namely | specifically | Three cities hosted the event; namely, Delhi, Mumbai, and Bangalore. |
| that is | in other words | He needs experience; that is, he must intern first. |
| in other words | to clarify | The plan is viable; in other words, it can work. |

#### SUMMARY / CONCLUSION

| Conjunctive Adverb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| in conclusion | to summarize | In conclusion, teamwork is essential. |
| in summary | to summarize briefly | In summary, the product is ready. |
| in short | briefly | In short, we failed the deadline. |
| to sum up | to summarize | To sum up, the quarter was successful. |
| overall | considering everything | Overall, the project was a success. |
| in brief | concisely stated | In brief, we need more funding. |

## 11.7 Common Conjunction Errors in Placement Exams

### Error 1: Using BOTH ... AND without parallelism

```
WRONG:  She is both an engineer and she works as a consultant.
                  ↑ noun           ↑ clause
CORRECT: She is both an engineer and a consultant.
                   ↑ noun          ↑ noun
```

### Error 2: Using NEITHER ... NOR with wrong verb agreement

```
WRONG:  Neither John nor his friends was present.
CORRECT: Neither John nor his friends WERE present. (friends is closer → plural)

WRONG:  Neither the results nor the report were ready.
CORRECT: Neither the results nor the report WAS ready. (report is closer → singular)
```

### Error 3: Using NOT ONLY ... BUT ALSO without parallelism

```
WRONG:  He not only won the competition but also getting a scholarship.
CORRECT: He not only won the competition but also got a scholarship.
                     ↑ past tense              ↑ past tense (parallel)
```

### Error 4: ALTHOUGH vs BUT — never use both together

```
WRONG:  Although she studied hard, but she failed.
CORRECT: Although she studied hard, she failed.
   OR:   She studied hard, but she failed.
```

> [!IMPORTANT]
> "Although...but" is a DOUBLE CONNECTOR error. You can only use ONE:
> - ALTHOUGH alone, OR
> - BUT alone
> NEVER both together!

### Error 5: BECAUSE vs BECAUSE OF

```
BECAUSE    = conjunction (followed by a clause — subject + verb)
BECAUSE OF = preposition (followed by a noun/noun phrase)

WRONG:  She was late because of she missed the bus.
CORRECT: She was late because she missed the bus. (clause follows)
CORRECT: She was late because of the traffic. (noun follows)
```

### Error 6: SINCE vs FOR (in perfect tenses)

```
SINCE = specific point in time (since 2020, since Monday, since last week)
FOR   = duration of time (for three years, for a week, for two hours)

WRONG:  She has worked here since three years.
CORRECT: She has worked here FOR three years.

WRONG:  He has been ill for Monday.
CORRECT: He has been ill SINCE Monday.
```

### Error 7: UNLESS vs IF NOT — No double negative

```
WRONG:  Unless you don't study, you will fail.
CORRECT: Unless you study, you will fail.
(UNLESS already means "IF NOT" — adding "don't" creates a double negative)
```

### Error 8: EITHER ... OR vs NEITHER ... NOR

```
WRONG:  Neither he or she was present.
CORRECT: Neither he NOR she was present.
(NEITHER must always be paired with NOR, not OR)

WRONG:  Either he nor she will attend.
CORRECT: Either he OR she will attend.
(EITHER must always be paired with OR, not NOR)
```

### Error 9: Comma before FANBOYS (or lack thereof)

```
WRONG:  She studied hard but she failed. (missing comma before BUT)
CORRECT: She studied hard, but she failed.

WRONG:  She is smart, and hardworking. (no comma needed — single phrase)
CORRECT: She is smart and hardworking.
```

### Error 10: HOWEVER used as a conjunction (comma splice)

```
WRONG:  She worked hard, however she did not succeed.
CORRECT: She worked hard; however, she did not succeed.
   OR:   She worked hard. However, she did not succeed.
```

## 11.8 Practice Questions — 25 Questions

**Q1. _____ she was exhausted, she continued working until midnight.**

(A) But
(B) Although
(C) However
(D) Therefore

**✅ Answer: (B) Although**

**📖 Explanation:** "Although" is a subordinating conjunction that introduces a contrast clause. The sentence has a main clause ("she continued working") and a contrast clause ("she was exhausted"). ALTHOUGH joins a dependent clause to the main clause.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** BUT is a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) that joins two independent clauses. It cannot begin a dependent clause attached to a main clause in this structure.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** HOWEVER is a conjunctive adverb, not a subordinating conjunction. It would require a semicolon: "She was exhausted; however, she continued."
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** THEREFORE shows cause-effect. The sentence shows contrast, not a result.

**📏 Rule:** ALTHOUGH + dependent clause (contrast/concession)
**⚡ Shortcut:** If the two ideas are opposites/surprising, use ALTHOUGH (start), BUT (middle), or HOWEVER (with semicolon).
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, Wipro

**Q2. He is _____ an excellent programmer _____ a skilled team leader.**

(A) either...or
(B) neither...nor
(C) both...and
(D) not...but

**✅ Answer: (C) both...and**

**📖 Explanation:** The sentence implies he has BOTH qualities — programmer AND team leader. "Both...and" expresses positive addition of two equal qualities.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Either...or" presents alternatives (one OR the other), not both simultaneously.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "Neither...nor" expresses that he is NEITHER — which makes the sentence negative and contradictory.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "Not...but" would mean "not a programmer, but a team leader" — which contradicts the sentence meaning.

**📏 Rule:** BOTH...AND = positive addition of two equal elements
**⚡ Shortcut:** Both = 2 things together; Either = 1 of 2; Neither = 0 of 2.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, Accenture, IBM

**Q3. _____ you leave now, you will miss the connecting flight.**

(A) If
(B) Unless
(C) Although
(D) Since

**✅ Answer: (B) Unless**

**📖 Explanation:** "Unless you leave now" = "If you do NOT leave now." The sentence warns of a consequence if the condition (leaving now) is not met. UNLESS = IF NOT.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "If you leave now, you will miss..." means leaving causes missing — the opposite of the intended meaning.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** ALTHOUGH introduces contrast, not condition.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** SINCE introduces reason or time, not condition.

**📏 Rule:** UNLESS = IF NOT (condition not fulfilled → consequence follows)
**⚡ Shortcut:** Replace UNLESS with "If you do NOT" — if the meaning still works, UNLESS is correct.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Wipro, Capgemini

**Q4. She not only completed the report _____ also presented it to the board.**

(A) but
(B) and
(C) yet
(D) however

**✅ Answer: (A) but**

**📖 Explanation:** "Not only...but also" is the complete correlative pair. The correct form is "not only X, but also Y."

**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** "Not only...and also" is not the correct pair. The standard correlative is "not only...but also."
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Not only...yet also" is not a standard correlative pair.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "Not only...however also" is grammatically incorrect.

**📏 Rule:** NOT ONLY...BUT ALSO is the complete, standard correlative pair
**⚡ Shortcut:** Memorize the pair whole: "NOT ONLY — BUT ALSO" — never split with other words.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** All placement companies

**Q5. The project was delayed; _____, the team delivered excellent quality.**

(A) therefore
(B) because
(C) nevertheless
(D) unless

**✅ Answer: (C) nevertheless**

**📖 Explanation:** The sentence shows a contrast: despite the delay (problem), the quality was good. NEVERTHELESS = despite that. The semicolon confirms we need a conjunctive adverb.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** THEREFORE shows cause-effect (result), not contrast. It would mean the delay caused good quality — illogical.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** BECAUSE is a subordinating conjunction, not a conjunctive adverb. It cannot follow a semicolon in this position.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** UNLESS is a conditional conjunction, not appropriate here.

**📏 Rule:** Conjunctive adverbs (however, nevertheless, therefore) follow a SEMICOLON, not just a comma.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Semicolon + conjunctive adverb + comma = standard punctuation pattern.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** IBM, Accenture, Infosys

**Q6. _____ the manager _____ the team members were aware of the new policy.**

(A) Both...or
(B) Neither...nor
(C) Either...and
(D) Not only...and

**✅ Answer: (B) Neither...nor**

**📖 Explanation:** "Neither...nor" means NONE of the mentioned parties were aware — consistent with a negative scenario being described. The manager was not aware, AND the team members were not aware.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** "Both...or" is not a valid correlative pair. BOTH pairs with AND.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** "Either...and" is not a valid pair. EITHER pairs with OR.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "Not only...and" is not a valid pair. NOT ONLY pairs with BUT ALSO.

**📏 Rule:** Valid pairs: Both+AND, Either+OR, Neither+NOR, Not only+BUT ALSO
**⚡ Shortcut:** Match the pairs like shoes — each has its fixed partner. Mismatching = wrong.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, IBM

**Q7. _____ although she worked hard, she did not receive a promotion.**

(A) But
(B) No correction needed — the sentence is wrong
(C) However
(D) The sentence is correct as written

**✅ Answer: (B) No correction needed — the sentence is wrong**

**📖 Explanation:** "Although...but" is a double connector error. ALTHOUGH and BUT cannot both appear in the same sentence. Either use "Although she worked hard, she did not receive a promotion" OR "She worked hard, but she did not receive a promotion."

**📏 Rule:** ALTHOUGH and BUT cannot co-exist in the same sentence.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Check: is there an ALTHOUGH at the start AND a BUT in the middle? If yes → error!
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Accenture, IBM

**Q8. She missed the meeting _____ of the heavy traffic on the highway.**

(A) due
(B) because
(C) owing
(D) on account

**✅ Answer: (A) due**

**📖 Explanation:** "Due to" is a prepositional phrase meaning "because of." It is followed by a noun phrase. Here, "the heavy traffic" is a noun phrase. "Due to" fits perfectly.

**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** BECAUSE is a conjunction followed by a clause (subject+verb). "Because of the traffic" would be correct but the question gives no "of" after BECAUSE as an option.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** OWING (owing to) is also valid but "owing" alone cannot take "of" — it takes "to." If the blank had "to" it would also work.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** "On account of" is correct meaning but "on account of" requires two words, and "on account" alone cannot take "of" separately here.

**📏 Rule:** DUE TO + noun phrase = BECAUSE OF + noun phrase
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS, Wipro, Cognizant

**Q9. She has been working at this company _____ three years.**

(A) since
(B) for
(C) during
(D) from

**✅ Answer: (B) for**

**📖 Explanation:** FOR is used with a DURATION (three years = a period of time). SINCE is used with a POINT IN TIME (since 2020, since Monday).

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** SINCE must be followed by a point in time, not a duration. "Since three years" is incorrect.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** DURING means "throughout the period of" — it doesn't work with perfect tenses showing ongoing states.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** FROM is used with TO (from...to) for ranges, not alone with durations.

**📏 Rule:** FOR + duration; SINCE + point in time (with Perfect Tenses)
**⚡ Shortcut:** If you can ask "HOW LONG?" → FOR. If you can ask "WHEN did it start?" → SINCE.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** All companies

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** ALTHOUGH is a conjunction followed by a clause (subject+verb), not a noun phrase.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** DESPITE + noun phrase (without "of") is correct. "Despite his skills" ✓. But "despite of" is wrong, and here the blank is followed by just a noun phrase making "despite" alone correct too — BUT option (D) is in spite of which is also correct and matches the noun phrase correctly.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** EVEN THOUGH is a conjunction requiring a full clause.

**📏 Rule:** IN SPITE OF + noun/noun phrase (preposition); EVEN THOUGH + clause (conjunction)

**Q23. _____ the files _____ the database backup was corrupted after the attack.**

(B) Either...or
(C) Neither...nor
(D) Not only...but also

**✅ Answer: (C) Neither...nor**

**📖 Explanation:** The sentence implies BOTH were corrupted — but if it says "was corrupted" (singular after proximity), and the meaning is "both were corrupted," then "not only...but also" or "both...and" would express positive addition of disaster. However, "neither...nor" meaning NEITHER was uncorrupted (i.e., both were corrupted) makes contextual sense too. But the KEY is: "Neither the files nor the database backup was corrupted" = nothing was corrupted. That contradicts "after the attack." So "Not only the files but also the database backup was corrupted" is logically better. Answer D.

Wait — re-reading: "_____ the files _____ the database backup was corrupted." This says WAS (singular — proximity: backup is closer). If using Neither...nor: "Neither the files nor the database backup WAS corrupted" = nothing was corrupted. If using "Not only...but also" = both were corrupted. The context "after the attack" implies destruction → both corrupted → (D) Not only...but also.

## 📊 Chapter 11 Key Takeaways

| Concept | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| FANBOYS | 7 coordinators: For And Nor But Or Yet So | She tried, but she failed. |
| ALTHOUGH + BUT | Never use together | ✗ Although...but / ✓ Although OR But |
| Neither...Nor | Both negative, proximity SVA | Neither he nor they WERE there. |
| Either...Or | Alternatives, proximity SVA | Either she or he IS here. |
| Both...And | Positive addition, plural verb | Both X and Y ARE present. |
| Not Only...But Also | Emphasis on both, inversion when fronted | Not only did she win... |
| Since vs For | Since = point; For = duration | Since 2020 / For 3 years |
| Unless | = If Not (no double negative) | Unless you try (NOT unless you don't try) |
| So That | Purpose | He studied so that he would pass. |
| Semicolon + Conjunctive Adverb | However, therefore, moreover need semicolons | She tried; however, she failed. |
| Because vs Because of | Because + clause / Because of + noun | Because she was late / Because of traffic |
| Whether vs If | Two alternatives = WHETHER | Ask WHETHER she will come OR not. |
