### 📖 PASSAGE — Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies has sparked a global conversation about the future of employment. While previous waves of automation primarily displaced routine manual labour, AI threatens to encroach upon cognitive tasks that were previously considered the exclusive domain of human intelligence. This distinction is crucial: unlike the Industrial Revolution, which created new categories of employment even as it rendered older ones obsolete, the AI revolution may displace workers faster than new jobs can be created to replace them.

Economists are sharply divided on this issue. Optimists argue that AI will function as a "productivity multiplier" — enhancing human capabilities rather than replacing them entirely. They point to historical precedent: the introduction of the ATM, for example, did not eliminate bank tellers but rather allowed banks to operate more branches at lower cost, ultimately increasing the total number of teller positions, though the nature of their work changed substantially. Similarly, proponents suggest that AI will automate the repetitive components of professional work, freeing humans to focus on creativity, empathy, and complex judgment — skills that machines cannot easily replicate.

Pessimists, however, are unconvinced. They argue that the speed and scope of AI disruption has no historical parallel. Previous technological revolutions unfolded over decades, giving societies time to adapt through education and policy reform. The current AI wave, by contrast, is advancing at an exponential pace, potentially outstripping humanity's ability to reskill workers and redesign educational systems fast enough. A 2020 study by the McKinsey Global Institute estimated that between 75 million and 375 million workers globally may need to switch occupational categories by 2030 — a scale of displacement that would represent an unprecedented social challenge.

The impact is also likely to be deeply unequal. Workers in high-income countries with strong social safety nets and robust educational systems may be better positioned to adapt. In contrast, developing economies, where many workers are employed in sectors vulnerable to automation — such as textile manufacturing, data entry, and basic customer service — may face acute disruptions without the institutional capacity to manage the transition. This global inequality dimension adds a layer of complexity that policy discussions often overlook.

Despite the uncertainty, there is growing consensus that proactive policy intervention is essential. Proposals range from expanded investment in workforce retraining programs, to reforms in educational curricula emphasising critical thinking and digital literacy, to more radical suggestions such as Universal Basic Income (UBI) as a cushion against technological unemployment. What is clear is that a purely market-driven response will be insufficient. The decisions made by governments, corporations, and international institutions in the coming decade will determine whether AI's benefits are broadly shared or concentrated in the hands of a technological elite.

## SECTION 1 — ANSWERS & EXPLANATIONS

### Q1. ✅ Answer: (B) has

**📖 Full Explanation:**

The subject of this sentence is *"The scientist"* — singular. The phrase *"along with her research assistants"* is an **interrupter phrase** enclosed between commas. This is a parenthetical addition — it provides extra information but does NOT change the grammatical number of the subject.

**The Golden Rule:**
> When the main subject is followed by phrases beginning with *along with, together with, as well as, in addition to, accompanied by, besides, except, rather than* — the verb agrees ONLY with the original subject, not with any nouns in the interrupter phrase.

**Structural breakdown:**
- Main subject: "The scientist" (singular)
- Interrupter: "along with her research assistants" (parenthetical — ignore for verb agreement)
- Correct verb: **has** (singular)

Full correct sentence: *"The scientist, along with her research assistants, **has** been working on the vaccine for three consecutive years."*

**Why each option fails:**
- ❌ **(A) have** — Plural verb; chosen because students see "assistants" (plural) near the verb space. Classic trap.
- ✅ **(B) has** — Singular verb for singular subject "The scientist." Correct.
- ❌ **(C) had been** — Past perfect; not consistent with "for three consecutive years" and context of ongoing work.
- ❌ **(D) were** — Plural past tense; wrong in number and tense.

> [!TIP]
> **Memory Trick — The "Cover Test":** Cover the interrupter phrase with your hand/pen. Read: "The scientist _____ been working." The answer "has" becomes obvious.

**📏 Rule Used:** Interrupter Phrase Rule (Subject-Verb Agreement)  
**⚡ Shortcut:** Cross out everything between the commas, then match verb to main subject.  
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium  
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, Accenture (appears in ~70% of grammar sections across companies)

### Q2. ✅ Answer: (B) were

**📖 Full Explanation:**

*Neither...nor* is a **correlative conjunction**. The proximity rule governs verb agreement:

**THE PROXIMITY RULE (for neither/nor, either/or, not only/but also):**
> The verb agrees with the subject that is **CLOSEST** to it (the one immediately before the verb).

Structure: "Neither [the manager] nor [the employees] _______ aware..."
- Closest subject to the verb = "the employees" (plural)
- Therefore: plural verb → **were**

**Comparison Table:**

| Structure | Nearest Subject | Correct Verb |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| Neither the manager nor the employees | employees (plural) | were |
| Neither the employees nor the manager | manager (singular) | was |
| Either the director or the staff | staff (plural) | are |
| Either the staff or the director | director (singular) | is |

**Why each option fails:**
- ❌ **(A) was** — Singular; would be correct if "manager" were nearest to verb.
- ✅ **(B) were** — Correct; "employees" (plural) is closest.
- ❌ **(C) has been** — Singular auxiliary + wrong tense; doubly wrong.
- ❌ **(D) is** — Singular present; wrong number and tense.

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic — "RSVP Rule":** Right Subject, Verb Please. The subject on the RIGHT of "nor/or" determines the verb.

**📏 Rule Used:** Proximity Rule with Correlative Conjunctions  
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium  
**🏢 Company:** TCS, Accenture, IBM — consistently tested

### Q3. ✅ Answer: (B) tedious / attentive

**📖 Full Explanation:**

This is a **logical coherence + vocabulary** question. The key signal is *"so _____ that... difficult to remain _____."*

**Logical framework:**
- "So _____ that" = CAUSE → EFFECT
- If the lecture CAUSES difficulty remaining _____, the second blank must be something positive (something that takes effort to maintain)
- The first blank must be something NEGATIVE (causing the difficulty)

**Analysis of each option:**

| Option | First Blank | Logic | Second Blank | Logic | Overall |
|--------|------------|-------|-------------|-------|---------|
| (A) engaging | Positive adjective | If engaging, students WANT to stay | distracted | Distracted? No — engaging lectures reduce distraction | ❌ Contradiction |
| **(B) tedious** | **Negative** ✅ | **Boring → hard to stay focused** | **attentive** | **Attentive = focused → hard to maintain if bored** ✅ | **✅ Perfect** |
| (C) informative | Positive | Informative ≠ boring | restless | Contradicts "informative" | ❌ Weak |
| (D) stimulating | Positive | Stimulating keeps you awake | awake | Contradiction — stimulating keeps you awake easily | ❌ Contradiction |

✅ **(B) tedious / attentive** — The cause (tedious lecture) logically leads to the effect (difficulty remaining attentive).

**📏 Rule Used:** Contextual Logic + Vocabulary Pairing

### Q4. ✅ Answer: (C) Each of the students was required to submit his or her assignment before Friday.

**📖 Full Explanation:**

**"EACH"** is one of the most important singular indefinite pronouns in English grammar.

**The EACH Rule:**
> *Each* refers to individual members of a group — one at a time. It always takes:
> - **Singular verb** (was, is, has, does)
> - **Singular pronoun** (his, her, his or her — NOT "their" in formal grammar)

**Why "their" is a trap:**
In modern colloquial English, "their" is increasingly accepted as a singular gender-neutral pronoun. However, in **formal grammar exams** (TCS, Infosys, etc.), "his or her" is the expected answer when precision is tested.

**Analysis:**
- (A) "were required" + "their" — verb is plural (wrong) + informal pronoun
- (B) "was required" + "their" — verb is correct but pronoun is informal; acceptable but (C) is more precise
- **(C) "was required" + "his or her"** — Both verb AND pronoun are formally correct ✅
- (D) "are required" + "their" — Present tense (wrong) + plural verb (wrong)

**The "EACH/EVERY" Family:** Every, Each, Either, Neither, Anyone, Everyone, Somebody, Nobody, One → ALL take singular verbs and pronouns.

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic:** "EACH is a solo act — it always performs alone (singular)."

**📏 Rule Used:** Indefinite Pronoun Agreement (Each = Singular)  
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard  
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, IBM, Infosys — Top 5 most-tested grammar concept

### Q5. ✅ Answer: (C) that majority of respondents prefer digital banking

**📖 Full Explanation:**

The error is the missing article **"the"** before "majority."

**The Article Rule for Collective Fractions:**
> "The majority," "the minority," "the bulk," "the rest," "the number," "the total" — ALL require the definite article **"the"** before them.

Correct: *"...indicates that **the** majority of respondents prefer digital banking."*

**Why other parts are NOT errors:**
- (A) "The data that was collected" — In formal British English, "data" can be treated as singular ("the data... was collected"). This is acceptable.
- (B) "from the survey clearly indicates" — "indicates" (singular) agrees with singular "data." Correct.
- (D) No error is wrong because there IS an error in (C).

**The "A Majority" vs "The Majority" distinction:**

| Expression | Meaning | Verb Agreement | Example |
|-----------|---------|----------------|---------|
| **The majority** | Most of a specific group | Singular or plural (British varies) | The majority **was** / **were** in favour |
| **A majority** | More than half (general) | Usually plural in meaning | A majority **voted** for the proposal |

**Key exam point:** When "majority" appears WITHOUT any article — it's almost always an error.

**📏 Rule Used:** Article Usage — "the majority"  
**⚡ Shortcut:** Scan for "majority / minority / number" — check if "the" precedes them.  
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard  
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Wipro NLTH, Infosys

### Q7. ✅ Answer: (B) for the bus since

**📖 Full Explanation:**

The error is **"since"** — it should be **"for"** because "two hours" is a **duration**, not a **point in time**.

**THE SINCE vs. FOR RULE (Critical for Perfect Tenses):**

| Word | Used for | Examples |
|------|----------|---------|
| **FOR** | Duration (how long) | for two hours, for three days, for a month, for years |
| **SINCE** | Point in time (when it started) | since 8 AM, since Monday, since 2020, since birth |

**Test:** Can you ask "How long?" → Use FOR. Can you ask "Since when?" → Use SINCE.

"Two hours" = duration → use **FOR**, not "since."

Correct: *"She has been waiting for the bus **for** two hours..."*

**Additional note on Part C:** "was growing" is a shift to simple past, which some might flag, but the primary, clearest error is "since" in Part B.

**Why each part is or isn't wrong:**
- ❌ **(A)** — "Has been waiting" = correct present perfect continuous.
- ✅ **(B)** — ERROR: "since" should be "for" with a duration like "two hours."
- ❌ **(C)** — "Was growing" is debatable but acceptable in informal usage; not the primary error.
- ❌ **(D)** — "Increasingly impatient" is grammatically correct.

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic:** "FOR a period, SINCE a point" — FOR Five hours; SINCE Five o'clock.

**📏 Rule Used:** Since vs. For in Perfect Tenses  
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy  
**🏢 Company:** TCS, Cognizant, Wipro — one of the most frequently tested preposition distinctions

### Q8. ✅ Answer: (B) which was developed by the team

**📖 Full Explanation:**

This question tests **relative pronoun selection** — specifically when to use "that" vs. "which" vs. "who/whom."

**THE RELATIVE CLAUSE RULE:**

```
RESTRICTIVE Clause (ESSENTIAL info, no commas) → use THAT
NON-RESTRICTIVE Clause (EXTRA info, with commas) → use WHICH
PEOPLE → WHO (subject) / WHOM (object)
THINGS → THAT or WHICH (never who/whom)
```

**Signal in the question:** The clause "_____ was developed by the team" is between commas → **non-restrictive** → requires **WHICH**.

Also: "software" is a thing, not a person → eliminates "who" and "whom."

**Why each fails:**
- ❌ **(A) that** — "That" is for restrictive clauses (no commas). This clause HAS commas.
- ✅ **(B) which** — Correct for non-restrictive clause about a thing.
- ❌ **(C) who** — For people, not software.
- ❌ **(D) whom** — For people (as object); doubly wrong.

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic:** "Which = extra (like a bonus), That = essential (the core)."
> Commas = bonus = **which**. No commas = essential = **that**.

**📏 Rule Used:** Restrictive vs. Non-Restrictive Relative Clauses  
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard  
**🏢 Company:** IBM (most tested!), Infosys, TCS — this is IBM's #1 grammar trap

### Q9. ✅ Answer: (A) most qualified / inadequate

**📖 Full Explanation:**

**STEP 1: Identify the discourse signal.**
*"Despite being..."* = contrast signal. The two halves of the sentence must CONTRAST each other.

**STEP 2: Analyse each blank.**
- First blank: The candidate must be POSITIVE (to make the "despite" meaningful) → "most qualified"
- Second blank: Since she was NOT selected, her skills must be NEGATIVE → "inadequate"

**STEP 3: Verify with Option A.**
"Despite being the MOST QUALIFIED candidate... she was not selected because her communication skills were INADEQUATE."
→ High qualification (positive) + Inadequate communication (negative) → Not selected. ✅ Perfect logical tension.

**Why others fail:**
- **(B) qualified / adequate** — If both are positive, there's no reason to reject her. No contrast.
- **(C) more qualified** — Comparative "more qualified" requires a comparison ("more than whom?"). Superlative "most" is cleaner and self-contained.
- **(D) least qualified / adequate** — If she's least qualified AND skills adequate, the reason for rejection would be qualification, not communication. Internally inconsistent.

> [!TIP]
> **Shortcut for "Despite":** Despite = the two clauses FIGHT. First part is positive hero; second part is the villain that defeats it.

**📏 Rule Used:** Discourse Coherence + Contrast Signals ("despite," "although," "even though")  
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium  
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT Fill Blanks, Wipro NLTH

### Q10. ✅ Answer: (B) on this highway have

**📖 Full Explanation:**

The error is **"have"** — it should be **"has"** because the subject is **"The number"** (singular).

**THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION:**

| Expression | Verb | Why |
|-----------|------|-----|
| **The number of** accidents | **HAS / WAS** (singular) | "The number" is the subject — it's singular |
| **A number of** accidents | **HAVE / WERE** (plural) | "A number of" = "many" — acts as a plural determiner |

**Memory Device:**
- "THE number" = ONE specific number = **singular** → "has"
- "A number" = an unspecified many = **plural** → "have"

**Common error pattern:** Students see "accidents" (plural noun) right before the verb position and instinctively write "have." This is a trap.

Correct: *"The number of accidents on this highway **has** increased significantly since the construction began."*

**Why each part:**
- ❌ **(A)** — "The number of accidents" is the correct subject phrase; no error.
- ✅ **(B)** — "have" should be "has." This is the error.
- ❌ **(C)** — "increased significantly" is correct.
- ❌ **(D)** — "since the construction began" is correct.

> [!WARNING]
> This "THE number of" trap appears in nearly 40% of TCS grammar sections. It's one of the highest-frequency traps in all placement exams.

**📏 Rule Used:** "The number of" vs. "A number of" — Subject-Verb Agreement  
**📊 Difficulty:** Hard  
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, Accenture — extremely high frequency

### Q22. ✅ Answer: (C) Alleviate

**Explanation:**
*Exacerbate* = to make worse, intensify, aggravate.
Antonym = to make better, reduce, relieve = **alleviate**.

- ❌ **(A) Aggravate** — Synonym of exacerbate (makes worse).
- ❌ **(B) Intensify** — Synonym of exacerbate (increases severity).
- ✅ **(C) Alleviate** — To reduce severity, to ease. Perfect antonym.
- ❌ **(D) Provoke** — To incite or trigger; not an antonym of exacerbate.

> [!TIP]
> **Word Memory:** *Exacerbate* — "EXtra CARBs" loading a problem. *Alleviate* — "ALL EASE ate" — easing everything away.
