## PASSAGE 1: The Psychology of Decision Making

The field of behavioural economics has fundamentally altered how economists and psychologists understand human decision-making. Unlike classical economic theory, which assumes that individuals act as perfectly rational agents who maximise utility based on complete information, behavioural economics demonstrates that human decisions are systematically influenced by cognitive biases, heuristics, and emotional states. The pioneering work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky established that humans do not weigh probabilities and outcomes in the mathematically optimal way that classical theory predicts.

One of the most influential concepts to emerge from this field is "loss aversion" — the empirically verified phenomenon that people feel the pain of losses approximately twice as intensely as they feel the pleasure of equivalent gains. This asymmetry has profound implications across domains ranging from investment decisions to healthcare choices to public policy design. For instance, people are significantly more motivated to act to prevent a loss of Rs. 1,000 than to secure an equivalent gain of Rs. 1,000, even though the objective financial outcome is identical.

Another central concept is the "availability heuristic" — the tendency to assess the probability of an event based on how easily examples come to mind. This explains why people often dramatically overestimate the risks of dramatic but rare events (such as plane crashes or shark attacks) while underestimating everyday risks (such as car accidents or cardiovascular disease). The vividness and emotional impact of dramatic events make them more "available" in memory, skewing risk perception.

Behavioural economists have also identified "choice architecture" as a powerful tool for guiding better decisions without restricting freedom of choice. By thoughtfully designing the environment in which choices are made — for instance, by making healthy food options more visible and accessible in a cafeteria — policymakers can nudge individuals toward better outcomes while preserving their autonomy. This approach, often called "libertarian paternalism," has been applied with measurable success in pension enrollment, organ donation, and energy conservation programs.

## PASSAGE 2: Ocean Acidification and Marine Ecosystems

The world's oceans have absorbed approximately 30% of the carbon dioxide (CO₂) emitted by human activities since the Industrial Revolution. While this has moderated the pace of climate change by preventing CO₂ from accumulating in the atmosphere, it has come at a severe cost to marine ecosystems. When CO₂ dissolves in seawater, it forms carbonic acid, which then dissociates to release hydrogen ions — a process that increases the acidity of the ocean. Since pre-industrial times, surface ocean pH has declined from approximately 8.2 to 8.1, representing a 26% increase in hydrogen ion concentration, a phenomenon known as ocean acidification.

This change, while seemingly modest in absolute terms, has significant biological consequences. Many marine organisms — including corals, oysters, mussels, sea urchins, and certain species of plankton — depend on calcium carbonate to build their shells and skeletons. As ocean acidity increases, the availability of carbonate ions (necessary for shell formation) decreases, making it harder and more energetically costly for these organisms to build and maintain their structures. In severe cases, existing shells may begin to dissolve.

The ecological implications extend far beyond individual species. Coral reefs, which cover less than 1% of the ocean floor but support approximately 25% of all marine species, are particularly threatened. As reefs bleach and erode due to both warming temperatures and acidification, the complex ecosystems they support collapse, with cascading effects up and down the food chain. Small planktonic organisms called pteropods, a critical food source for fish, whales, and seabirds, have already been observed with dissolving shells in some of the most acidified waters of the Southern Ocean.

The rate of current ocean acidification is estimated to be ten times faster than any period in the last 50 million years, giving marine species insufficient time to adapt through natural evolutionary processes. Addressing this crisis requires not merely improving the resilience of individual ecosystems but fundamentally reducing the CO₂ emissions that drive both climate change and acidification simultaneously.

## PASSAGE 3: The Rise of the Gig Economy

The gig economy — characterised by short-term contracts, freelance work, and platform-mediated engagements rather than permanent employment — has grown dramatically over the past decade. Enabled by digital platforms such as Uber, Airbnb, Upwork, and Fiverr, the gig economy now encompasses hundreds of millions of workers worldwide. Proponents argue that it offers unprecedented flexibility and autonomy, allowing individuals to work on their own terms, pursue multiple income streams, and develop diverse skill sets without the constraints of traditional nine-to-five employment.

Critics, however, point to significant vulnerabilities that gig workers face. Unlike traditional employees, most gig workers do not receive employee benefits such as health insurance, paid leave, retirement contributions, or unemployment protection. They are classified as independent contractors, which means the companies that depend on their labour bear no legal responsibility for their welfare. During economic downturns or health crises — as vividly demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic — gig workers are often the first and most severely impacted, with no income replacement or institutional safety net.

The regulatory landscape is evolving in response to these concerns. Several jurisdictions, including California with its landmark AB5 legislation and the United Kingdom following its Supreme Court ruling on Uber, have moved to reclassify gig workers with greater protections. However, implementation has been contested vigorously by platform companies, who argue that reclassification would destroy the flexibility that workers themselves value and make their business models economically unviable. The debate centres on a fundamental tension: how to extend meaningful labour protections to a growing workforce without eliminating the genuine benefits of flexible work arrangements.

## SECTION B — SENTENCE CORRECTION ANSWERS

---

### Q16. ✅ Answer: (B) has decided

**Explanation:**
*"Board of directors"* is a **collective noun**. In American English (used in most Indian placement exams), collective nouns take **singular verbs** when acting as a unified body.

- ❌ **(A) have decided** — Treats "board" as plural; British usage but not preferred in exam context
- ✅ **(B) has decided** — Singular collective noun → singular verb
- ❌ **(C) had been decided** — Passive + past perfect; wrong form
- ❌ **(D) were deciding** — Past continuous + plural; doubly wrong

**Rule:** Collective nouns (board, committee, team, jury, staff, faculty) + singular verb in formal/exam English.

---

### Q17. ✅ Answer: (B) was

**Explanation:**
*"Neither of the two"* → "Neither" is singular (it refers to each one individually, not collectively). Singular subject → singular verb → **was**.

**Key pattern:** Neither / Either + of + plural noun → SINGULAR verb
- "Neither of them **was** present." ✅
- "Either of the options **is** acceptable." ✅

*"The team's performance"* — the subject is **"performance"** (singular, uncountable noun), not "team" or "team's."

### Q19. ✅ Answer: (B) who work

**Explanation:**
This is the **"one of those who"** construction — one of the most tricky SVA patterns.

**"She is one of those employees WHO WORK diligently."**

The relative clause "who _____ diligently" refers to **"employees"** (plural), not to "she" or "one."

**Logic:** Among ALL those employees who work diligently, she is one. The verb in the relative clause agrees with "employees" (plural) → **work**.

**Compare:**
- "She is ONE of those employees WHO WORK diligently." (many employees work; she is one of them)
- "She is THE ONLY ONE of those employees WHO WORKS diligently." (only she works diligently)

- ❌ (A) "who works" — Wrong; refers to "she" (singular), not "employees"
- ✅ (B) "who work" — Correct; refers to plural "employees"
- ❌ (C) "that works" — "That" can be used but "works" still wrong
- ❌ (D) "whom works" — "Whom" is object pronoun; can't be subject of "works"

### Q20. ✅ Answer: (B) as well as the annexures, was

"As well as" is an **interrupter phrase** (like "along with," "together with"). The main subject is **"The report"** (singular) → singular verb → **was**.

- ❌ (D) "and the annexures, was" — "and" would join two subjects = plural → need "were", not "was"

**Note:** If the conjunction were "AND" (not "as well as"), the verb would be "were" because "and" joins two subjects.

### Q23. ✅ Answer: (C) As it was a rainy day

**Explanation:**
The original sentence has a **dangling modifier**: "Being a rainy day, the management cancelled..."

This implies the MANAGEMENT is a rainy day (the subject of "cancelled" must match the subject of "being"). This is grammatically incorrect.

To fix a dangling modifier, either:
1. Add a proper subject to the modifier: "**It being a rainy day**..." (B) — acceptable but slightly formal
2. Convert to a full subordinate clause: "**As it was a rainy day**..." (C) — cleaner and preferred

**(C)** is the most natural and clear correction.

- ❌ (A) — Dangling modifier error; unchanged from the question
- ❌ (B) — Grammatically valid but awkward; (C) is more natural
- ✅ (C) — Clean, natural correction using a subordinate clause
- ❌ (D) — "The rainy day being" — still an awkward dangling phrase

### Q24. ✅ Answer: (C) submit

**Explanation:**
*"Insisted that"* triggers the **SUBJUNCTIVE MOOD**.

**Subjunctive Rule:** After verbs of demand, request, insistence, suggestion, recommendation + "that" → use bare infinitive (base form of verb) for ALL subjects.

Verbs that trigger subjunctive: **insist, demand, suggest, recommend, request, require, propose, urge**

Structure: **[Subject] insisted/demanded/suggested that [subject] + [base verb]**

"The manager insisted that every employee **submit**..." (not "submits," not "submitted")

- ❌ (A) "submits" — Indicative mood; wrong after "insisted that"
- ❌ (B) "submitted" — Past tense; wrong mood
- ✅ (C) "submit" — Correct subjunctive (base form)
- ❌ (D) "would submit" — Modal; wrong in subjunctive context

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The Subjunctive after "insist/demand/suggest that" is one of Infosys's TOP tested grammar concepts. Memorise: "that + bare infinitive" for ALL subjects.

---

### Q25. ✅ Answer: (B) smarter

**Explanation:**
"More smarter" is a **double comparative error**. You cannot use BOTH "more" AND the "-er" suffix.

Choose one:
- "smarter" (comparative with -er suffix) ✅
- "more intelligent" (comparative with "more" for longer words) ✅

"More smarter" uses both → grammatically wrong.

- ❌ (A) "more smarter" — Double comparative error
- ✅ (B) "smarter" — Correct simple comparative
- ❌ (C) "most smartest" — Double superlative error
- ❌ (D) "more smart" — "Smart" is a one-syllable word; use "-er" form ("smarter"), not "more"

### Q26. ✅ Answer: (A) had barely finished — (Original is CORRECT)

**Explanation:**
"Had barely finished...when" is a standard **past perfect structure** indicating one past action completed just before another.

Structure: **[Subject] had barely/scarcely/hardly [past participle] when [simple past]**

"The committee **had barely finished** reviewing...when the chairman announced..."

This is grammatically perfect. No change needed.

- ✅ (A) — Original is correct
- ❌ (B) "has barely finished" — Present perfect; wrong tense
- ❌ (C) "barely had finished" — Unusual word order; "had barely" is standard
- ❌ (D) "barely finished" — Simple past; loses the past perfect sequence contrast

### Q27. ✅ Answer: (B) Despite his

**Explanation:**
*"Despite"* is a preposition and is NEVER followed by "of."

**Common Error:** "Despite of" — This is WRONG. It's an Indian English error.

- "Despite" = preposition, directly followed by noun/pronoun/gerund
- "In spite of" = correct (note: "in spite" DOES need "of")

Correct forms:
- "**Despite** his credentials..." ✅
- "**In spite of** his credentials..." ✅
- "Despite **of** his credentials..." ❌

- ❌ (A) "Despite of his" — "Of" after "despite" = error
- ✅ (B) "Despite his" — Correct
- ❌ (C) "In spite his" — Missing "of" (should be "in spite OF")
- ❌ (D) "Inspite of his" — "Inspite" is not a word; it's "in spite" (two words)

> [!WARNING]
> "Despite of" is one of the most common Indian English errors. It appears in nearly every Infosys and TCS error detection section.

### Q28. ✅ Answer: (A) whom — (Original is CORRECT)

**Explanation:**
*"Most of whom"* — "whom" is the object form of the relative pronoun "who."

Test: Replace "whom/who" with "him/her" (object pronoun) or "he/she" (subject pronoun).
- "Most of them had never visited" → "them" = object pronoun → use **whom**. ✅

- ✅ (A) "whom" — Correct; object pronoun in "most of whom"
- ❌ (B) "which" — For things, not people
- ❌ (C) "who" — Subject pronoun; wrong in "most of ___" construction
- ❌ (D) "that" — Cannot follow a preposition or "of"; used only in restrictive clauses

### Q29. ✅ Answer: (B) lay

**Explanation:**
This tests the confusable verbs **lie vs. lay** (one of the most-tested and most-confused verb pairs in English).

| Verb | Meaning | Past Tense | Past Participle |
|------|---------|------------|----------------|
| **Lie** (intransitive) | To recline/rest | **lay** | lain |
| **Lay** (transitive) | To place something | **laid** | laid |

The sentence: "She _______ on the grass" → intransitive (no object after it) → verb = **lie**.
Past tense of **lie** = **lay**.

Correct: "She **lay** on the grass and stared at the clouds."

- ❌ (A) "laid" — Past tense of "lay" (transitive); but this sentence is intransitive
- ✅ (B) "lay" — Past tense of "lie" (intransitive). Correct.
- ❌ (C) "lain" — Past participle of "lie"; needs auxiliary ("had lain")
- ❌ (D) "lied" — Past tense of "lie" meaning "to tell a falsehood"; wrong meaning entirely

> [!TIP]
> **Lie/Lay Trick:** "A chicken LAYS eggs (transitive — lays something). A person LIES down (intransitive — no object)."

### Q30. ✅ Answer: (A) was — (Original is CORRECT)

**Explanation:**
*"The news"* — "news" is an **uncountable noun** and always takes a singular verb.

"The news of the merger **was** received..." ✅

Other uncountable nouns that are often mistaken for plural: news, information, furniture, advice, luggage, equipment, knowledge, evidence, homework.

All take singular verbs.

- ✅ (A) "was" — Correct; "news" = singular uncountable
- ❌ (B) "were" — Plural; wrong for uncountable "news"
- ❌ (C) "have been" — Plural auxiliary; wrong
- ❌ (D) "had" — Wrong tense context
