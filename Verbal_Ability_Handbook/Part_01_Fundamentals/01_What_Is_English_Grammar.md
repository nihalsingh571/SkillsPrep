## 1.1 What Is Language?

Language is not merely a collection of words. It is the most sophisticated system of communication ever developed by any species on Earth. It is the vehicle through which ideas, emotions, intentions, and knowledge are transmitted between human beings across time and space.

### 1.1.1 The Definition of Language

```
LANGUAGE = A structured system of communication that uses symbols
(words, signs, sounds) governed by rules (grammar) to convey meaning.
```

Every language in the world, whether it is Hindi, Tamil, Mandarin, Arabic, or English, shares the same fundamental properties:

| Property | Definition | Example in English |
|---|---|---|
| **Arbitrariness** | No natural connection between a word and its meaning | The word "dog" doesn't look like a dog |
| **Productivity** | Infinite new sentences can be created | You can form sentences no one has ever said before |
| **Displacement** | Language can refer to things not present | "Yesterday I went to Delhi" |
| **Cultural transmission** | Language is learned, not inherited | Children learn whatever language they are raised in |
| **Duality of patterning** | Sounds combine to form words; words combine to form meaning | /k/ + /ae/ + /t/ = "cat" |
| **Recursion** | Sentences can be embedded inside other sentences | "The man who said that the girl who smiled won the prize was wrong" |

### 1.1.2 The Three Layers of Language

To truly understand grammar, you must understand that language operates on three simultaneous levels:

```
+--------------------------------------------------+
|                   LANGUAGE                       |
|                                                  |
|  FORM         MEANING          USE               |
|  (Grammar)    (Semantics)      (Pragmatics)      |
|  How it's     What it          How it's          |
|  structured   means            used in context   |
+--------------------------------------------------+
```

- **Form (Grammar/Syntax)**: The rules governing how words are arranged. "I am going" is correct form; "I going am" is wrong form.
- **Meaning (Semantics)**: What the words and sentences mean. "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" has correct form but no meaning.
- **Use (Pragmatics)**: How language is used in real situations. "Can you pass the salt?" is technically a question but pragmatically a request.

> [!NOTE]
> Placement tests primarily test **Form** (grammar rules) and **Meaning** (vocabulary, reading comprehension). Understanding why these rules exist makes them far easier to remember and apply.

### 1.1.3 Why English?

English is the primary language of:
- International business and commerce
- Software documentation and technical communication
- Academic research and publishing
- The Internet (over 60% of web content is in English)
- Global aviation, medicine, and diplomacy

This is precisely why every major Indian IT company — TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture, HCL — tests English communication ability as a core placement criterion. They know that their employees will write emails to clients in the USA, UK, Australia, and Singapore. They know that poorly written emails cost contracts. Grammar is not academic; it is professional currency.

## 1.2 What Is Grammar?

### 1.2.1 The Simple Definition

```
GRAMMAR = The set of structural rules that govern the composition of
clauses, phrases, and words in a natural language.
```

But this definition is too dry. Let us approach it differently.

**Grammar is to language what rules are to a game.** In cricket, you cannot score runs by throwing the ball into the stumps yourself. In English, you cannot place an adjective after a noun (in most cases). The game works because everyone follows the same rules. Communication works because speakers follow the same grammatical rules.

### 1.2.2 Descriptive vs. Prescriptive Grammar

This distinction is crucial and often tested conceptually in MBA entrance exams.

| Type | What It Does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive Grammar** | Describes how people actually speak/write | "He don't know" is valid if people say it |
| **Prescriptive Grammar** | Prescribes how people *should* speak/write | "He doesn't know" is the correct form |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Placement examinations follow **prescriptive grammar** — the "textbook correct" form. Even if something sounds natural in casual speech, you must know the formal, prescriptive rule.

**Example:**
- Casual speech: "Between you and I, this is wrong." (sounds correct to many)
- Prescriptive grammar: "Between you and **me**" (correct — object of preposition must be objective case)

### 1.2.3 The Four Components of Grammar

Grammar is not one thing — it is a system of four interlocking components:

```
+----------------------------------------------------------------+
|                    GRAMMAR COMPONENTS                          |
|                                                                |
|  PHONOLOGY   MORPHOLOGY    SYNTAX        SEMANTICS             |
|  (sounds)    (word forms)  (arrangement) (meaning)             |
|                                                                |
|  /th/ in     walk+ed       "She sings"   "sings" means        |
|  "thought"   walk+ing      NOT "Sings    to produce vocal      |
|              walk+er       she"          musical sounds        |
+----------------------------------------------------------------+
```

**1. Phonology** — The study of sounds. This matters in pronunciation (verbal communication tests, GD-PI rounds).

**2. Morphology** — The study of word forms and word building. Understanding morphology helps with vocabulary:
- `un-` (prefix) + `happy` (root) + `-ness` (suffix) = `unhappiness`
- `inter-` + `national` = `international`
- `bio-` + `logy` = `biology`

**3. Syntax** — The study of sentence structure. This is the CORE of what placement tests call "grammar." Syntax tells us:
- Word order (Subject + Verb + Object)
- How phrases connect
- How clauses relate

**4. Semantics** — The study of meaning. This overlaps with vocabulary and reading comprehension tests.

> [!TIP]
> **Memory Trick (PMSS):** "**P**lease **M**ake **S**entences **S**ensible" — Phonology, Morphology, Syntax, Semantics.

### 1.2.4 The Eight Parts of Speech — The Building Blocks

Every word in the English language belongs to one of eight categories called **Parts of Speech**:

| # | Part of Speech | What It Does | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Noun** | Names a person, place, thing, or idea | *book, Delhi, honesty* |
| 2 | **Pronoun** | Replaces a noun | *he, she, it, they* |
| 3 | **Verb** | Shows action or state | *run, is, seem* |
| 4 | **Adjective** | Describes a noun/pronoun | *tall, beautiful, three* |
| 5 | **Adverb** | Describes a verb/adjective/adverb | *quickly, very, well* |
| 6 | **Preposition** | Shows relationship between words | *in, on, at, under* |
| 7 | **Conjunction** | Joins words/phrases/clauses | *and, but, because* |
| 8 | **Interjection** | Expresses sudden emotion | *Oh! Wow! Alas!* |

> [!TIP]
> **Memory Trick:** "**N**o **V**isitor **A**rrives **A**fter **P**olice **C**heck **I**dentity" — Noun, Verb, Adjective, Adverb, Preposition, Conjunction, Interjection (+ Pronoun replaces Noun).

> [!WARNING]
> **The same word can be different parts of speech depending on how it is used:**
> - "I need a *light* jacket." — *light* = Adjective
> - "Please *light* the candle." — *light* = Verb
> - "Switch on the *light*." — *light* = Noun
>
> This context-dependence is frequently tested in placement exams!

## 1.3 Why Grammar Matters in Placements

Consider these two emails from two different engineering graduates:

**Email A (Poor Grammar):**
> "Sir, I wants to inform you that the project which we was working on have been completed. Kindly do the needful."

**Email B (Good Grammar):**
> "Dear Sir, I would like to inform you that the project we have been working on has been completed. Please review it at your earliest convenience."

Both communicate the same information. But Email A signals:
- Lack of professional training
- Potential communication failures with international clients
- Risk to the company's reputation

This is why companies screen candidates for English ability even before technical rounds.

## 1.4 A Brief History of English Grammar

Understanding where English grammar comes from gives you insight into *why* certain rules seem strange or inconsistent.

### 1.4.1 The Origins — Old English (450–1150 AD)

Old English (also called Anglo-Saxon) was brought to Britain by Germanic tribes — the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes — after the Roman withdrawal in the 5th century.

Old English looked almost nothing like modern English:
- Old English: *"Hwat! We Gardena in geardagum..."* (Opening of Beowulf)
- Modern Translation: "Listen! We have heard of the glory of the kings of the Spear-Danes in days of yore..."

**Key feature:** Old English used *case endings* (like Latin) to show grammatical function. Nouns changed form depending on whether they were subjects, objects, or possessors.

### 1.4.2 Middle English (1150–1500 AD)

The Norman Conquest of 1066 changed everything. French-speaking Normans ruled England, and French vocabulary flooded into English. This is why English has pairs like:

| Anglo-Saxon Word | French/Latin Word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *ask* | *inquire* | to request information |
| *begin* | *commence* | to start |
| *end* | *terminate* | to finish |
| *freedom* | *liberty* | state of being free |
| *kingly* | *royal* | relating to a king |
| *gut* | *courage* | bravery |
| *help* | *assist* | to aid |
| *holy* | *sacred* | religiously dedicated |

> [!NOTE]
> This is why English has so many synonyms! The native Germanic word and the imported French/Latin word both survived. Vocabulary tests in placements often use the more formal, Latinate words — precisely the ones borrowed from French and Latin.

### 1.4.3 Early Modern English (1500–1700 AD)

The printing press (1476 in England) standardized spelling. The King James Bible (1611) and Shakespeare's works (1590s–1610s) established conventions that we still follow today.

This is the era that gave us:
- "You" replacing "thou/thee" (though subjunctive "were" with singular subjects remains)
- Standardized verb conjugations
- Fixed sentence order (SVO — Subject-Verb-Object)

### 1.4.4 Modern English (1700 AD–Present)

Modern English grammar was largely codified by 18th-century grammarians like Robert Lowth (*A Short Introduction to English Grammar*, 1762) and Lindley Murray. These scholars often imposed Latin rules on English, which is why we have "rules" like:
- "Don't split infinitives" (e.g., "to boldly go" was considered wrong)
- "Don't end a sentence with a preposition" (though Winston Churchill famously mocked this)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Many "old rules" that placement tests use are based on these 18th-century prescriptive standards. Even if modern linguists reject them, they appear in Indian placement exams. You must know both: what is "technically correct" for tests AND what modern usage permits.

### 1.4.5 Indian English — Common Errors

Indian English has its own unique features — many of which are grammatically "incorrect" by prescriptive standards but are extremely common:

| Indian English (Common usage) | Standard English (Prescriptive) | Error Type |
|---|---|---|
| "Do the needful" | "Please do what is required" | Archaic/vague expression |
| "Revert back to me" | "Reply to me" (revert = go back already) | Redundancy |
| "Myself Rahul" | "I am Rahul" / "My name is Rahul" | Wrong pronoun use |
| "I am having a headache" | "I have a headache" | Stative verb in continuous form |
| "She is knowing the answer" | "She knows the answer" | Stative verb in continuous form |
| "What is your good name?" | "What is your name?" | Cultural calque from Hindi |
| "He passed out from college" | "He graduated from college" | Wrong idiom |
| "He is more smarter" | "He is smarter" | Double comparative |
| "Please intimate me" | "Please inform me" | Wrong word (intimate ≠ inform) |
| "I will come back soon only" | "I will come back soon" | Discourse particle "only" misuse |

> [!WARNING]
> These Indian English forms are tested as **errors** in placement exams. You must recognize them as incorrect and know the standard form. They appear especially in TCS, Infosys, and Accenture tests.

## 1.5 The Grammar Tree — Hierarchy of Language

This is the most important conceptual framework in this entire book. Every grammar topic you will study fits somewhere in this hierarchy.

### 1.5.1 The Complete Language Hierarchy

```
+----------------------------------------------------------------------+
|                      LANGUAGE HIERARCHY                              |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 1:  MORPHEMES (smallest units of meaning)                    |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Free morphemes: stand alone -> "cat", "run", "happy" |
|            +-- Bound morphemes: need a base -> "-ed", "un-", "-ing"  |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 2:  WORDS (Parts of Speech)                                  |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Nouns (book, happiness, Delhi)                        |
|            +-- Verbs (run, is, believe)                              |
|            +-- Adjectives (tall, beautiful, three)                   |
|            +-- Adverbs (quickly, very, never)                        |
|            +-- Pronouns (he, she, they, it)                          |
|            +-- Prepositions (in, on, at, under)                      |
|            +-- Conjunctions (and, but, because)                      |
|            +-- Interjections (Oh! Wow!)                              |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 3:  PHRASES (group of words WITHOUT subject+finite verb)     |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Noun Phrase: "the tall man in the blue coat"          |
|            +-- Verb Phrase: "has been running rapidly"               |
|            +-- Adjective Phrase: "very happy with the results"       |
|            +-- Adverb Phrase: "quite early in the morning"           |
|            +-- Prepositional Phrase: "under the old oak tree"        |
|            +-- Participial Phrase: "Running quickly, she tripped"    |
|            +-- Gerund Phrase: "Swimming every day builds stamina"    |
|            +-- Infinitive Phrase: "To win the race is his goal"      |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 4:  CLAUSES (group of words WITH subject + finite verb)      |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Independent Clause: "She works hard"                  |
|            +-- Dependent Clause:                                     |
|                +-- Noun clause: "that she works hard"                |
|                +-- Adjective clause: "who works hard"                |
|                +-- Adverb clause: "because she works hard"           |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 5:  SENTENCES (complete thought with S + V)                  |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Simple: One independent clause                        |
|            +-- Compound: 2+ independent clauses                      |
|            +-- Complex: 1 independent + 1+ dependent clause          |
|            +-- Compound-Complex: 2+ ind. + 1+ dep. clauses           |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 6:  PARAGRAPHS (group of related sentences)                  |
|            |                                                         |
|            +-- Topic sentence (main idea)                            |
|            +-- Supporting sentences (evidence, examples)             |
|            +-- Concluding sentence (wrap-up or transition)           |
|                                                                      |
|  LEVEL 7:  TEXT / DISCOURSE (connected paragraphs)                  |
|            +-- Essays, articles, reports                             |
|            +-- Tested via: Reading Comprehension                     |
|            +-- Tested via: Para Jumbles, Para Completion             |
+----------------------------------------------------------------------+
```

### 1.5.2 Understanding Morphemes (Level 1)

A **morpheme** is the smallest meaningful unit of language. Unlike letters (which have no independent meaning), morphemes carry meaning.

**Types of Morphemes:**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Free morpheme** | Can stand alone as a word | *cat, run, happy, book* |
| **Bound morpheme — Prefix** | Attached to the beginning | *un-happy, re-write, pre-view* |
| **Bound morpheme — Suffix** | Attached to the end | *happi-ness, walk-ed, teach-er* |
| **Bound morpheme — Infix** | Inserted within (rare in English) | Expletive infixes in informal speech |

**Vocabulary Power from Morphemes:**

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *un-* | not | unhappy, unclear, undo |
| *re-* | again | rewrite, redo, return |
| *pre-* | before | preview, predate, prepare |
| *mis-* | wrongly | misunderstand, mislead |
| *dis-* | not/opposite | disagree, disappear |
| *inter-* | between | international, interview |
| *sub-* | under | submarine, substandard |
| *super-* | above | supernatural, supervise |
| *anti-* | against | antibiotic, antisocial |
| *pro-* | in favor of | pro-democracy, promote |
| *in-/im-/ir-/il-* | not | incorrect, impossible, irregular, illegal |
| *over-* | too much | overestimate, overwork |
| *under-* | too little | underestimate, underperform |
| *trans-* | across | transport, transform |
| *mono-* | one | monologue, monochrome |
| *bi-* | two | bilingual, bicycle |
| *tri-* | three | triangle, trilingual |
| *poly-* | many | polygamy, polygon |
| *micro-* | small | microscope, microchip |
| *macro-* | large | macroeconomics, macroscopic |

| Suffix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| *-tion/-sion* | act/state of | education, revision |
| *-ness* | state of being | happiness, darkness |
| *-ment* | result/act of | achievement, movement |
| *-ful* | full of | beautiful, helpful |
| *-less* | without | careless, hopeless |
| *-er/-or* | one who does | teacher, doctor |
| *-able/-ible* | capable of | readable, possible |
| *-ive* | having quality of | creative, effective |
| *-ous* | characterized by | dangerous, famous |
| *-ly* | in the manner of | quickly, beautifully |
| *-ity* | quality/state | creativity, ability |
| *-ism* | doctrine/belief | capitalism, terrorism |
| *-ist* | one who practices | capitalist, artist |
| *-ize/-ise* | to make/become | modernize, realise |
| *-fy* | to make | simplify, magnify |

> [!TIP]
> **Vocabulary Superpower:** Knowing just 30 prefixes and 30 suffixes allows you to decode the meaning of thousands of unfamiliar words in exams — without memorizing each word individually.

### 1.5.3 From Words to Sentences — The Building Process

Let's trace how a sentence is built from morphemes up:

```
MORPHEME LEVEL:
  [re] + [organize] + [ation]   -->   "reorganization"

WORD LEVEL:
  "The" (Article/Adjective)
  "company" (Noun)
  "has" (Auxiliary Verb)
  "completed" (Main Verb - past participle)
  "its" (Possessive Pronoun)
  "reorganization" (Noun)

PHRASE LEVEL:
  "The company"           --> Noun Phrase (Subject)
  "has completed"         --> Verb Phrase
  "its reorganization"    --> Noun Phrase (Object)

CLAUSE LEVEL:
  "The company has completed its reorganization"
  ^ This is an INDEPENDENT CLAUSE (subject + verb + complete thought)

SENTENCE LEVEL:
  "The company has completed its reorganization."
  ^ This is a SIMPLE SENTENCE (one independent clause)

EXTENDED SENTENCE:
  "The company has completed its reorganization, and it is now ready
   to compete globally."
  ^ This is a COMPOUND SENTENCE (two independent clauses joined by "and")
```

### 1.5.4 The Paragraph Level

A well-structured paragraph has:

```
PARAGRAPH STRUCTURE
======================================================

[TOPIC SENTENCE]      --> States the main idea
       |
[SUPPORTING SENTENCE 1] --> Provides evidence/example
       |
[SUPPORTING SENTENCE 2] --> Elaborates further
       |
[SUPPORTING SENTENCE 3] --> Additional evidence
       |
[CONCLUDING SENTENCE] --> Wraps up or transitions
```

> [!NOTE]
> Reading Comprehension questions test your ability to identify the topic sentence (main idea), supporting details, tone, inference, and author's purpose. Understanding paragraph structure makes RC dramatically easier.

### 1.5.5 Discourse and Text Cohesion Devices

At the highest level (discourse), sentences and paragraphs are connected using:

| Cohesion Device | Type | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Reference** | Pointing to something already mentioned | *he, she, it, they, this, that, these, those* |
| **Substitution** | Replacing a word/phrase | *"I like coffee. So do I."* |
| **Ellipsis** | Omitting understood words | *"Who did this?" "I [did this]."* |
| **Conjunction** | Signaling relationships | *however, therefore, moreover, but, and* |
| **Lexical cohesion** | Using related words | Synonyms, repetition, collocations |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Para jumble questions test your understanding of cohesion devices. When you see a pronoun (he/she/it/they) at the start of a sentence, the previous sentence must have introduced the antecedent. This is a powerful para jumble solving technique.

## 1.6 How Companies Test Grammar

### 1.6.1 The Five Question Types

Every verbal question in every placement exam falls into one of these five categories:

| Question Type | What It Tests | Format |
|---|---|---|
| **Error Detection** | Identify which part of a sentence has a grammatical error | 4 underlined parts, choose the error |
| **Sentence Correction** | Choose the grammatically correct version | Select the best sentence among 4 options |
| **Fill in the Blanks** | Choose the correct word/phrase for the blank | Single or double blank with 4 choices |
| **Reading Comprehension** | Understand a passage and answer questions | 3-5 questions per passage |
| **Para Jumbles** | Arrange jumbled sentences into a coherent paragraph | 4-6 sentences, choose the correct order |

### 1.6.2 Error Detection — How It Works

You are given a sentence with 4 underlined parts, and you must identify which part has an error.

**Example:**
> Riya and her sister **(A)** was playing **(B)** in the garden **(C)** when it **(D)** started raining.

**Analysis:**
- (A) "Riya and her sister" — Compound subject (correct)
- (B) "was playing" — WRONG! Compound subject "Riya AND her sister" = plural → should be "**were** playing"
- (C) "in the garden" — Prepositional phrase (correct)
- (D) "started raining" — Past tense (correct)

**Answer: (B)**

### 1.6.3 Sentence Correction — How It Works

You are given a sentence (often with an underlined portion) and must choose the correct alternative.

**Example:**
> He is one of the students who **has** passed the examination.

(A) has passed  
(B) have passed  
(C) had passed  
(D) passing  

**Answer: (B) have passed**

**Explanation:** "One of the students who" — The relative clause "who" refers to "students" (plural), not "one" (singular). Therefore, the verb must be plural: "have passed."

> [!WARNING]
> **The "One of the..." Trap:** This is one of the most frequently tested patterns in TCS and Infosys exams. Always ask: what does the relative pronoun "who/which/that" refer to?
> - "He is **one** of the students who **have** passed." (who = students, plural)
> - "He is the **only one** of the students who **has** passed." (who = one, singular)

### 1.6.4 Fill in the Blanks — Common Patterns

| Pattern Tested | Example | Correct Answer | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Article | "She is __ honest person." | *an* | an before vowel sound /h/ is silent |
| Preposition | "I am good __ mathematics." | *at* | *at* for skills and subjects |
| Tense | "By tomorrow, she __ the report." | *will have finished* | Future perfect for completed future action |
| Conjunction | "He is poor __ honest." | *but* | *but* for contrast |
| Modal | "You __ submit the form today." | *must* | *must* for strong obligation |
| Correlative | "Neither the teacher __ the students came." | *nor* | neither...nor pair |

### 1.6.5 Para Jumbles — How to Solve Them

Para jumbles require you to arrange 4–6 sentences into a logical paragraph. The strategy:

```
PARA JUMBLE STRATEGY
======================================================

STEP 1: Find the OPENING sentence
        --> No pronoun reference to an unknown antecedent
        --> Introduces a topic/idea freshly
        --> Often has a general statement
        --> Does NOT begin with "however," "therefore," "but"

STEP 2: Find the CLOSING sentence
        --> Uses conclusive language: "therefore", "thus", "finally"
        --> Summarizes or wraps up the topic
        --> Often the most general statement

STEP 3: Link remaining sentences using:
        --> Pronoun references (he/she/it/they -> who does it refer to?)
        --> Demonstratives (this/that/these/those -> what do they point to?)
        --> Transition words (however, moreover, furthermore, consequently)
        --> Logical flow (cause before effect, problem before solution)
        --> Time sequence (first, then, finally, later)

STEP 4: Use PAIR-LINKING
        --> Find two sentences that MUST go together
        --> Eliminate options that separate these pairs
```

### 1.6.6 Reading Comprehension Question Types

| RC Question Type | What It Asks | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Main Idea** | What is the passage primarily about? | Read topic sentences of each paragraph |
| **Specific Detail** | What does the passage say about X? | Locate the relevant paragraph, read carefully |
| **Inference** | What can be inferred from the passage? | Must be supported by the passage, not general knowledge |
| **Tone/Attitude** | What is the author's tone? | Look for emotional/evaluative language |
| **Vocabulary in context** | What does the word X mean as used here? | Use context clues, not dictionary meaning |
| **Title** | What would be the best title? | Similar to main idea — choose most comprehensive option |
| **Assumption** | What assumption does the author make? | Look for unstated premises |

**Q1. Which of the following sentences contains an error in subject-verb agreement?**

(A) She doesn't know the answer.  
(B) He don't know the answer.  
(C) They don't know the answer.  
(D) We don't know the answer.  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** "He" is a third-person singular subject. With third-person singular subjects, the auxiliary verb is "doesn't" (not "don't"). "Don't" is used with I, you, we, they.

**Why (A) is correct:** "She doesn't" — correct third-person singular form.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "He don't" — incorrect. Third-person singular requires "doesn't."  
**Why (C) is correct:** "They don't" — "they" is plural, so "don't" is correct.  
**Why (D) is correct:** "We don't" — "we" is plural, so "don't" is correct.

**Rule:** Subject-Verb Agreement — Third-person singular present tense verbs add -s/-es; auxiliary "do" becomes "does."  
**Shortcut:** If subject is he/she/it (or singular noun), always use "does/doesn't/is/has."  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, Infosys, Accenture

**Q2. The word "light" in "She needs a light coat for the summer" is used as a/an ___.**

(A) Noun  
(B) Verb  
(C) Adjective  
(D) Adverb  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** "Light" here describes the noun "coat" — it tells us what kind of coat. A word that describes/modifies a noun is an adjective.

**Why (A) is wrong:** A noun would name a person/place/thing — "light" is not naming anything here.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** A verb shows action — "light" is not showing any action here.  
**Why (C) is correct:** "Light" describes the noun "coat" — it is an adjective.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb — "light" modifies the noun "coat."

**Rule:** The function in the sentence determines the part of speech, not the word itself.  
**Shortcut:** Ask "What question does the word answer?" — adjectives answer "What kind?" "Which one?" "How many?"  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** Infosys, Wipro

**Q3. Which of the following is a BOUND morpheme?**

(A) cat  
(B) run  
(C) un-  
(D) happy  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** A bound morpheme cannot stand alone as a word — it must be attached to another morpheme. "un-" cannot be used alone (you can't say "She is un"); it must attach to a base word (un+happy, un+clear, un+do).

**Why (A) is wrong:** "cat" is a free morpheme — it stands alone as a complete word.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "run" is a free morpheme — it stands alone.  
**Why (C) is correct:** "un-" is a prefix — a bound morpheme that cannot stand alone.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "happy" is a free morpheme — it stands alone.

**Rule:** Free morphemes stand alone; bound morphemes (prefixes/suffixes) must attach to a base.  
**Shortcut:** If you can use it as a standalone word in a sentence, it's a free morpheme.  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** IBM, Capgemini

**Q4. "She is knowing the answer." — What type of error is this?**

(A) Subject-Verb Agreement error  
(B) Stative verb used in continuous tense (Indian English error)  
(C) Wrong pronoun used  
(D) Tense error (should be past tense)  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** "Know" is a stative verb (expresses a state of mind, not an action). Stative verbs are NOT used in the continuous/progressive tense in standard English. The correct form is "She knows the answer." Using "knowing" in the continuous tense is a characteristic Indian English error.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Subject-verb agreement is fine — "She" + "is" is grammatically matched.  
**Why (B) is correct:** Using stative verbs in continuous form is the error.  
**Why (C) is wrong:** "She" is the correct pronoun here.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** Present tense is appropriate for this context.

**Rule:** Stative verbs (know, believe, love, hate, want, need, understand, own, possess, see, hear, smell, taste) are NOT used in continuous tenses.  
**Shortcut:** KUSBAL — Know, Understand, See, Believe, Appear, Like = Stative = no "-ing" form in continuous.  
**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Company:** TCS, Infosys, Accenture, Cognizant

**Q5. Identify the INDEPENDENT CLAUSE in: "Although she studied hard, she failed the exam."**

(A) Although she studied hard  
(B) she failed the exam  
(C) Although she studied  
(D) hard, she failed  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** An independent clause contains a subject and verb AND expresses a complete thought — it can stand alone as a sentence. "She failed the exam" can stand alone. "Although she studied hard" has a subject and verb but is introduced by "although" — a subordinating conjunction that makes it dependent.

**Why (A) is wrong:** "Although she studied hard" is a dependent/subordinate clause — "although" makes it dependent.  
**Why (B) is correct:** "She failed the exam" is a complete, independent thought.  
**Why (C) is wrong:** Still part of the dependent clause introduced by "although."  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "hard, she failed" is not a proper clause — it's an awkward fragment.

**Rule:** A dependent clause begins with a subordinating conjunction (although, because, when, if, unless, until, while, since, as, though) and cannot stand alone as a sentence.  
**Shortcut:** Remove the first word. If what remains makes full sense, the original clause was dependent.  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, IBM

**Q6. Which sentence uses PRESCRIPTIVE grammar correctly?**

(A) It is me who made the mistake.  
(B) It is I who made the mistake.  
(C) It is mine who made the mistake.  
(D) It is myself who made the mistake.  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** After the verb "to be" (is, are, was, were), prescriptive grammar requires the subjective (nominative) case. "It is I" is grammatically correct even though "It is me" is common in spoken English.

**Why (A) is wrong:** "me" is objective case — prescriptively incorrect after "to be," though common in speech.  
**Why (B) is correct:** "I" is subjective case — prescriptively correct after linking verb "is."  
**Why (C) is wrong:** "mine" is possessive — doesn't fit this context grammatically.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "myself" is reflexive/emphatic — should only be used when the subject also appears in the same clause.

**Rule:** Pronouns after "to be" (linking verbs) take the subjective/nominative case: I, he, she, we, they.  
**Shortcut:** "It is I" — the verb "is" works like "="; both sides must be subjective. "It = I."  
**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Company:** Infosys, IBM

**Q7. "Run!" is an example of which sentence type?**

(A) Declarative  
(B) Interrogative  
(C) Imperative  
(D) Exclamatory  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** An imperative sentence gives a command, instruction, or request. The subject "you" is implied (not stated). "Run!" commands someone to run.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Declarative sentences make statements ("She runs fast.").  
**Why (B) is wrong:** Interrogative sentences ask questions ("Does she run fast?").  
**Why (C) is correct:** Imperative sentences give commands. The implied subject is "you."  
**Why (D) is wrong:** Exclamatory sentences use the structure "What a/an...!" or "How...!" to express strong emotion.

**Rule:** Sentence type is determined by PURPOSE, not punctuation. An imperative can end with ! or . An exclamatory uses "What" or "How" as an exclamation starter.  
**Shortcut:** DIIE — Declarative (statement), Interrogative (question), Imperative (command), Exclamatory (What/How + emotion).  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, Wipro

**Q8. "The company completed its reorganization." — What is the DIRECT OBJECT?**

(A) The company  
(B) completed  
(C) its reorganization  
(D) reorganization  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** The direct object is what receives the action of the verb. "Completed" is the verb. What did the company complete? "Its reorganization." The complete noun phrase including "its" is the direct object.

**Why (A) is wrong:** "The company" is the subject — it performs the action.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "completed" is the verb — it shows the action.  
**Why (C) is correct:** "its reorganization" is the complete noun phrase functioning as the direct object.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "reorganization" alone is only the head noun, not the complete object.

**Rule:** Direct Object = noun/pronoun that receives the action of the verb. Ask: "Subject + Verb + WHAT/WHOM?"  
**Shortcut:** S + V + O pattern. Company (S) + completed (V) + its reorganization (O).  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** Accenture, Capgemini

**Q9. Which sentence is COMPOUND?**

(A) The cat sat on the mat.  
(B) Although it was raining, we went outside.  
(C) She sang beautifully at the concert last night.  
(D) She sang, and he danced.  

**Answer: (D)**

**Explanation:** A compound sentence consists of two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) or a semicolon. "She sang, and he danced" has two independent clauses joined by "and."

**Why (A) is wrong:** One independent clause — simple sentence.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** One dependent clause + one independent clause — complex sentence.  
**Why (C) is wrong:** One independent clause — simple sentence.  
**Why (D) is correct:** Two independent clauses ("She sang" + "he danced") joined by "and" — compound sentence.

**Rule:** Compound = Independent + Coordinating Conjunction + Independent. FANBOYS = For And Nor But Or Yet So.  
**Shortcut:** Look for FANBOYS joining two clauses that can each stand alone as sentences.  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, Infosys

**Q10. Which is the CORRECT way to introduce yourself?**

(A) Myself Ravi.  
(B) I am Ravi.  
(C) Me is Ravi.  
(D) Mine is Ravi.  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** "Myself Ravi" is a common Indian English error. "Myself" (reflexive pronoun) should only be used when the subject of the sentence also refers to oneself ("I hurt myself") or for emphasis ("I myself did it"). It cannot replace "I am."

**Why (A) is wrong:** "Myself Ravi" — incorrect use of reflexive pronoun as a subject complement.  
**Why (B) is correct:** "I am Ravi" — correct subject + linking verb + noun (subject complement).  
**Why (C) is wrong:** "Me" is objective case — cannot be the subject of a sentence.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "Mine" is possessive — "mine is Ravi" is nonsensical.

**Rule:** Reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself, ourselves) must refer back to the subject of the same clause. They cannot replace "I" as a subject.  
**Shortcut:** "Myself" is only correct when "I" (or the subject) already appears in the same clause.  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, Accenture, Infosys

**Q11. In "He is one of the students who have passed," why is "have" (plural) used?**

(A) Because "He" is the antecedent of "who"  
(B) Because "students" is the antecedent of "who"  
(C) Because "one" is always followed by a plural verb  
(D) Because "passed" requires a plural auxiliary  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** The relative pronoun "who" refers to its nearest logical antecedent, which is "students" (plural), not "one" (singular). Therefore, the verb must agree with "students" — "have passed."

**Why (A) is wrong:** "He" is the subject of the main clause, not the antecedent of "who."  
**Why (B) is correct:** "Who" refers to "students" — the noun it logically modifies.  
**Why (C) is wrong:** "One" takes a singular verb in the main clause ("is"), but the relative clause verb agrees with the antecedent of "who."  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "Passed" doesn't control whether the auxiliary is singular or plural.

**Rule:** In "one of the [plural noun] who/that + verb," the verb agrees with the plural noun. Exception: "The ONLY one of the students who HAS passed" — singular.  
**Shortcut:** Ask: "Who passed — one person, or all the students?" — All students passed the exam. Therefore plural.  
**Difficulty:** Hard  
**Company:** TCS, Infosys, IBM

**Q12. Which of the following is a GERUND phrase?**

(A) Running fast, he fell.  
(B) To run fast is his goal.  
(C) Running every morning is healthy.  
(D) He runs every morning.  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** A gerund phrase uses a verb+ing form as a NOUN (subject, object, or complement). In (C), "Running every morning" is the subject of the sentence — it functions as a noun.

**Why (A) is wrong:** "Running fast" modifies "he" (tells us what he was doing as he fell) — this is a PARTICIPIAL phrase (adjective function).  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "To run fast" is an INFINITIVE phrase (noun function here, but infinitive, not gerund).  
**Why (C) is correct:** "Running every morning" is the subject — GERUND phrase (noun function).  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "He runs every morning" — "runs" is a finite verb in a regular clause.

**Rule:** Gerund = verb+ing functioning as a NOUN. Participle = verb+ing functioning as an ADJECTIVE.  
**Shortcut:** Replace the phrase with "IT." If the sentence makes sense, it's a gerund. "IT is healthy." — gerund confirmed.  
**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Company:** Infosys, IBM

**Q16. Which sentence follows PRESCRIPTIVE grammar for the "who/whom" rule?**

(A) Who did you speak to?  
(B) To whom did you speak?  
(C) Whom did you speak to?  
(D) With who did you speak?  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** Prescriptive rules: (1) Don't end a sentence with a preposition. (2) Use "whom" (not "who") as the object of a preposition. "To whom did you speak?" follows both rules perfectly.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Ends with preposition (prescriptively incorrect) + uses "who" instead of "whom."  
**Why (B) is correct:** Preposition "to" placed before "whom"; "whom" is correct object case.  
**Why (C) is wrong:** Ends with preposition "to" (prescriptively incorrect), though "whom" is correct.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "Who" should be "whom" (object of preposition "with").

**Rule:** In prescriptive grammar: (1) Place prepositions before their objects at the sentence start. (2) Use "whom" as the object of a verb or preposition.  
**Shortcut:** Replace with "him/her" — if "him/her" fits, use "whom." "You spoke to HIM" = whom.  
**Difficulty:** Hard  
**Company:** IBM, Infosys

**Q17. In which sentence is "that" used as a SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTION?**

**Why (A) is wrong:** "That" is a demonstrative adjective modifying "book."  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "That" is a relative pronoun referring back to "book."  
**Why (C) is correct:** "That" introduces a noun clause as a subordinating conjunction.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "That" is a demonstrative pronoun.

**Rule:** Conjunction "that" introduces noun clauses after verbs of thinking, saying, knowing, believing, feeling.

**Q18. Which word has the suffix correctly applied?**

(A) Readible  
(B) Teachible  
(C) Accessible  
(D) Workible  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** The suffix "-ible" attaches to Latin-derived roots, while "-able" attaches to English/Germanic roots or free morphemes. "Access" comes from Latin, so it takes "-ible": accessible. "Read," "teach," and "work" are English free morphemes: readable, teachable, workable.

**Why (A) is wrong:** "Read" is English free morpheme — readable (not readible).  
**Why (B) is wrong:** "Teach" is English free morpheme — teachable (not teachible).  
**Why (C) is correct:** "Accessible" is correct — Latin root "access" + -ible.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "Work" is English free morpheme — workable (not workible).

**Rule:** "-able" for English/Germanic roots (when the root is a standalone word); "-ible" for Latin roots (root is not a standalone English word).  
**Shortcut:** Can you use the root as a standalone word? Yes (read, teach, work) = "-able." No (access alone gives "access" but the adjective form needs "-ible") = "-ible."

**Q19. What type of word is "however" in: "I wanted to go; however, it rained."?**

(A) Coordinating conjunction  
(B) Subordinating conjunction  
(C) Conjunctive adverb  
(D) Preposition  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** "However" is a conjunctive adverb. It connects two independent clauses and shows the relationship (contrast), but unlike a coordinating conjunction, it must be preceded by a semicolon (or period) and followed by a comma.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Coordinating conjunctions are FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so). "However" is not a FANBOYS word.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** Subordinating conjunctions introduce dependent clauses. "However" does not make a clause dependent.  
**Why (C) is correct:** "However" is a conjunctive adverb — it connects clauses adverbially.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** Prepositions show relationships between nouns/pronouns.

**Rule:** Conjunctive adverbs: however, therefore, moreover, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, meanwhile, otherwise. Always use semicolon before and comma after.  
**Shortcut:** If you can move the word to different positions in the second clause without losing grammaticality, it's a conjunctive adverb.  
**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Company:** IBM, Infosys

**Q20. "Revert back to me with the details." — What error does this sentence contain?**

**Why (C) is wrong:** "Details" (plural) is correct here.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** The sentence does have an error.

**Rule:** Avoid tautological/redundant expressions: "revert back," "return back," "past history," "future plans," "end result," "completely finish," "advance forward," "free gift."  
**Shortcut:** "Revert" = Re (back) + vert (turn) = to turn back. Already contains the concept.

**Q21. Which sentence uses the correct NATURAL (SVO) word order?**

(A) To the market went she.  
(B) She to the market goes.  
(C) She goes to the market.  
(D) Goes she to the market.  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** Standard English follows Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order in declarative sentences. "She" (S) + "goes" (V) + "to the market" (adverbial/object) follows the natural pattern.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Inverted order (Adverbial + V + S) — unusual, only for poetic/emphasis.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** S + Adverbial + V — incorrect order (verb must immediately follow subject).  
**Why (C) is correct:** She (S) + goes (V) + to the market (complement/adverbial) — correct SVO.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** V + S + Adverbial — interrogative order, but this is a statement.

**Rule:** English default declarative word order: Subject + Verb + Object/Complement/Adverbial.  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Company:** TCS, Wipro

**Q22. Which of the following demonstrates DISPLACEMENT in language?**

(A) Saying "ouch!" when hurt  
(B) Using the word "dog" to refer to a dog  
(C) Describing events from last year  
(D) Forming a new sentence never said before  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** Displacement means the ability of language to refer to things not present in time or space. Describing past events (last year) demonstrates displacement — the events are not happening right now or here.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Saying "ouch" is an immediate response — no displacement. This demonstrates the sign-meaning connection.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** Using "dog" for a dog shows arbitrariness (no natural connection between word form and meaning).  
**Why (C) is correct:** Past events are displaced in time — talking about them demonstrates displacement.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** Forming new sentences demonstrates productivity.

**Rule:** Properties of language: Arbitrariness, Productivity, Displacement, Cultural Transmission, Duality of Patterning, Recursion.  
**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Company:** MBA entrance exams (CAT, XAT, MAT)

**Q23. "Time flies like an arrow" is famous for being syntactically ambiguous. How many interpretations does it have?**

(A) 1  
(B) 2  
(C) 3  
(D) 4  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** This sentence has at least THREE syntactic interpretations:
1. "Time" (S) + "flies" (V) + "like an arrow" (adverb) = Time passes as quickly as an arrow.
2. "Time" (V: to time) + "flies" (N: the insects) + "like an arrow" (adverb) = Measure flies' speed in the manner of an arrow.
3. "Time-flies" (compound noun, type of fly) + "like" (V) + "an arrow" (O) = Time-flies are fond of an arrow.

**Rule:** Syntactic ambiguity = same string of words, multiple grammatical parse trees, different meanings.  
**Shortcut:** Always check whether a word can be a different part of speech.  
**Difficulty:** Expert  
**Company:** CAT, MBA entrance exams  

---

**Q24. "Neither the students nor the teacher ___ present." — Choose the correct verb form.**

(A) were  
(B) was  
(C) are  
(D) have been  

**Answer: (B)**

**Explanation:** With "neither...nor" (and "either...or"), the verb agrees with the subject CLOSEST to the verb. Here, "the teacher" (singular) is closest to the verb. Therefore, singular: "was."

**Why (A) is wrong:** "were" is plural — but closest subject "teacher" is singular.  
**Why (B) is correct:** "was" agrees with "the teacher" (singular, nearest to verb).  
**Why (C) is wrong:** "are" is present tense plural — incorrect.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** "have been" is plural — incorrect.

**Rule:** Neither/Nor and Either/Or — Verb agrees with the NEAREST subject (Proximity Rule).  
**Shortcut:** Cover everything before "nor/or" and just look at the noun right before the verb.  
**Difficulty:** Hard  
**Company:** TCS, Infosys, IBM, Accenture

**Q25. The sentence "She said that he knew that the report which had been submitted that day would be reviewed" contains which language property?**

(A) Displacement  
(B) Arbitrariness  
(C) Recursion  
(D) Cultural transmission  

**Answer: (C)**

**Explanation:** The sentence embeds clauses within clauses ("that he knew" within "she said"; "that the report" within "he knew"; "which had been submitted" within "the report"). This infinite embedding capacity is called RECURSION.

**Why (A) is wrong:** Displacement refers to referring to absent things (past/future/elsewhere) — not about embedding.  
**Why (B) is wrong:** Arbitrariness refers to the lack of natural connection between word form and meaning.  
**Why (C) is correct:** Recursion allows clauses to be embedded inside other clauses, creating complex structures.  
**Why (D) is wrong:** Cultural transmission refers to language being learned, not inherited.

**Rule:** Recursion = The ability of language to embed structures within structures, allowing for theoretically infinite sentence generation.  
**Shortcut:** If you can keep adding "who said that she knew that..." indefinitely, that's recursion.  
**Difficulty:** Expert  
**Company:** CAT, GRE, MBA entrance exams
