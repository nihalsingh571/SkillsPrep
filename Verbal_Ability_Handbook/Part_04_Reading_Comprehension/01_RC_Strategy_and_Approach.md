## 1.1 Why RC is Different in Placements

Most students approach Reading Comprehension the way they read a textbook — linearly, word by word, trying to understand everything. This approach is **catastrophically wrong** for placement exams.

Here is the brutal truth about placement RC:

1. **You are NOT tested on how well you understood the passage.** You are tested on whether you can locate, interpret, or infer specific information within a strict time limit.
2. **The passage is a reference document, not a story.** You return to it; you don't memorize it.
3. **Trap answers are engineered.** Every wrong option is designed to sound plausible to someone who read the passage carelessly.
4. **Speed and accuracy must co-exist.** You have roughly 90 seconds per question including reading time.

> [!WARNING]
> The single most common mistake in RC: Reading the full passage deeply and then trying to answer questions from memory. This wastes time AND leads to errors because memory distorts meaning.

### The Placement RC Mindset Shift

| ❌ Old Mindset | ✅ New Mindset |
|---|---|
| "I must understand every word" | "I must know WHERE information is" |
| "Read once, answer from memory" | "Read strategically, return to passage" |
| "Speed OR accuracy" | "Speed WITH accuracy via method" |
| "The longest option is more detailed" | "The correct option is precise, not verbose" |
| "I'll skip difficult vocabulary" | "I'll use context clues to decode it" |

## 1.2 The 4-Step RC Method: Preview → Read → Recall → Answer

This method is used by top scorers in GMAT, GRE, CAT, and placement exams alike. It is scientifically grounded in cognitive psychology — specifically in how working memory processes and retains information.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              THE 4-STEP RC METHOD                   │
│                                                     │
│  STEP 1: PREVIEW     (20-30 seconds)                │
│  ↓ Read questions FIRST (not options)               │
│  ↓ Identify question types                          │
│  ↓ Note what to look for while reading              │
│                                                     │
│  STEP 2: READ        (60-90 seconds)                │
│  ↓ Skim for structure                               │
│  ↓ Underline keywords                               │
│  ↓ Note paragraph functions                         │
│                                                     │
│  STEP 3: RECALL      (10 seconds)                   │
│  ↓ Look away from passage                           │
│  ↓ Mentally summarize in one sentence               │
│  ↓ Identify author's main point and tone            │
│                                                     │
│  STEP 4: ANSWER      (30 seconds per Q)             │
│  ↓ Predict answer before reading options            │
│  ↓ Eliminate using scope test                       │
│  ↓ Return to passage if needed (LOCATE, don't guess)│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Step 1: PREVIEW — Reading Questions First

**Why read questions before the passage?**

When you read questions first, your brain activates "active search mode." As you read the passage, your brain **automatically flags** relevant information. This is the same reason you find your own name in a noisy crowd — your brain is pre-programmed to search for it.

**How to Preview:**
1. Read only the **question stem** (the question itself), NOT the options.
2. Options are traps at this stage — they plant wrong ideas in your mind.
3. Classify each question by type:
   - "Which of the following best describes the main idea?" → Main Idea question
   - "According to the passage..." → Specific Detail question
   - "The author implies..." → Inference question
   - "The word X in line Y most nearly means..." → Vocabulary-in-context question
   - "The author's tone can be described as..." → Tone question

4. Make a mental (or physical) note of **keywords** in each question.

**Example Preview:**
> Passage followed by 5 questions. After previewing:
> - Q1: Main idea → Look for the central argument while reading
> - Q2: "carbon emissions" → Flag this term while reading
> - Q3: Inference about "corporate responsibility" → Understand author's stance
> - Q4: Tone → Pay attention to adjectives and verb choices
> - Q5: True/False → Look for exact factual claims

Now when you read the passage, you are NOT reading passively — you are hunting for specific prey.

### Step 2: READ — Strategic Skimming with Annotation

**The passage is a map. You need the landmarks, not every pebble.**

During the Read step, you are looking for:
1. **The main idea** (usually in the first paragraph, first or last sentence)
2. **The function of each paragraph** (states problem / gives example / provides solution / concludes)
3. **Key terms and keywords** (nouns, cause-effect words, contrast words)
4. **The author's stance** (for/against/neutral)

**What to annotate (mentally or in margins):**
- First sentence of each paragraph: underline or mark
- Contrast words: however, but, yet, nevertheless, although, despite
- Support words: because, since, therefore, hence, thus
- Example indicators: for instance, for example, such as, like
- Conclusion indicators: therefore, in conclusion, ultimately, hence

> [!TIP]
> **The FIRST and LAST paragraphs are goldmines.** The first paragraph introduces the topic and often states the thesis. The last paragraph wraps up the argument. Read these slightly more carefully than the middle paragraphs.

### Step 3: RECALL — The 10-Second Compression

After reading, before looking at options, do this:

**Put the passage in one sentence.**

Example: *"The author argues that AI, while transformative for business, poses significant ethical risks that require regulatory intervention."*

This 10-second step:
- Solidifies your understanding of the main idea
- Tells you the tone (argumentative, critical, neutral)
- Gives you a benchmark to test answer choices against

If you cannot summarize the passage in one sentence, you have NOT understood it well enough. Re-skim the first and last paragraphs.

### Step 4: ANSWER — The Predict-Eliminate-Confirm Method

**PREDICT:** Before reading options, state what you think the answer is.
**ELIMINATE:** Remove options that are:
  - Too broad (beyond the scope of the passage)
  - Too narrow (only one small detail)
  - Contradictory to the passage
  - Extreme language (always, never, all, none, completely)
**CONFIRM:** The remaining option should match your prediction.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> If two options both seem correct, **the more specific and precisely worded one is usually correct.** Wrong answers often use correct words from the passage in incorrect combinations.

## 1.3 Skimming at 300+ Words Per Minute

Average reading speed: 150-200 WPM. Expert skim speed: 400-600 WPM. Your placement target: **300+ WPM** with 70%+ comprehension.

### What Skimming Is (and Is NOT)

**Skimming IS:**
- Reading the first and last sentence of every paragraph fully
- Reading topic sentences (usually the first sentence) carefully
- Running your eyes quickly over the middle sentences for keywords
- Slowing down at signal words (however, therefore, because, but)

**Skimming is NOT:**
- Skipping paragraphs entirely
- Reading only 20% of the text randomly
- Speed-reading every word equally fast
- Losing comprehension for the sake of speed

### The FLAIR Skimming Technique

```
F - First sentence of each paragraph (read fully)
L - Last sentence of each paragraph (read fully)
A - Any signal/transition words in between
I - Important keywords (nouns, key terms from questions)
R - Return only if needed for specific questions
```

### Building Speed: The Eye-Stop Method

Your eyes don't read smoothly — they **jump and stop** (called saccades and fixations). Slow readers stop at every word. Fast readers stop every 3-5 words.

**Exercise:** Train your eyes to take in **word groups**, not individual words:
- Slow: The / company / decided / to / invest / heavily / in / renewable / energy
- Fast: "The company" / "decided to invest" / "heavily in renewable energy"

Practice this by placing your finger under text and moving it at a pace slightly faster than comfortable. Your eyes will follow, forcing faster processing.

### Signal Words That Slow You Down vs Speed You Up

| Signal Word Type | Words | Reading Action |
|---|---|---|
| **SLOW DOWN** (key point coming) | However, but, yet, although, surprisingly, critically | Read fully |
| **SLOW DOWN** (conclusion) | Therefore, thus, hence, consequently, in conclusion | Read fully |
| **SPEED UP** (examples) | For example, for instance, such as, like | Skim quickly |
| **SPEED UP** (elaboration) | Furthermore, moreover, additionally, also | Note, don't deeply read |
| **SLOW DOWN** (contrast) | On the other hand, in contrast, conversely | Read fully |

> [!TIP]
> **Mnemonic: "BEST slows you down"**
> - **B**ut
> - **E**xcept
> - **S**urprisingly / **S**till
> - **T**herefore / **T**hus
> These words signal turning points — slow down and read carefully.

## 1.4 The Keyword-Underlining Method

### Why Underlining Works

When you physically (or mentally) underline keywords, you:
1. Force yourself to identify what is important
2. Create a visual index for quick retrieval
3. Engage actively rather than passively with the text
4. Speed up return visits to the passage for specific questions

### What to Underline

```
RULE: Underline NOUNS more than VERBS. Underline SPECIFIC terms more than GENERAL ones.
```

| Type | Examples | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Proper nouns | Names, countries, organizations, people | HIGH |
| Technical terms | GDP, mitochondria, blockchain, IoT | HIGH |
| Numbers and statistics | 45%, 2019, 3.2 billion | HIGH |
| Contrast markers | However, but, despite | HIGH |
| Conclusion markers | Therefore, thus, hence | HIGH |
| Vague nouns | "things," "aspects," "factors" | LOW — don't underline |
| Filler phrases | "It is important to note that," "needless to say" | DON'T underline |

### Practical Underlining in Placement Exams

In online tests (TCS NQT, Infosys Hackwithinfy), you may not be able to physically underline. Instead:
- **Mental flagging:** As you read, consciously note the location of key facts ("carbon emissions is in paragraph 2, line 3")
- **Scratch paper:** Jot 3-4 word notes per paragraph: P1=AI benefits, P2=ethical risks, P3=regulation
- **Question-mapping:** After reading each question, write the paragraph number where the answer likely lives

## 1.5 How to Handle Difficult Vocabulary in Passages

### Strategy 1: Context Clues (Most Reliable)

Words don't exist in isolation. The surrounding sentence almost always gives you enough clues.

**Types of context clues:**

**1. Definition clues** — the author defines the word directly:
> "The company adopted a parsimonious, or extremely thrifty, approach to spending."
> → Parsimonious = extremely thrifty

**2. Synonym clues** — a synonym appears nearby:
> "The CEO was known for her loquacious, talkative nature in interviews."
> → Loquacious ≈ talkative

**3. Antonym clues** — a contrast word signals the opposite meaning:
> "Unlike the verbose report that filled 200 pages, the new summary was concise."
> → Verbose ≠ concise → verbose = wordy/long

**4. Example clues** — examples hint at the meaning:
> "Several phenomena, such as earthquakes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions, are classified as seismic events."
> → Seismic = relating to earth disturbances

**5. Mood/Tone clues** — the emotional context suggests meaning:
> "The politician's calumnious speech shocked the nation."
> → Shocking → probably negative → calumnious = making false, harmful statements

### Strategy 2: Word Roots (Second Line of Defense)

| Root | Meaning | Example Words |
|---|---|---|
| mal- | bad | malicious, malevolent, malign |
| bene- | good | beneficial, benevolent, benign |
| -ology | study of | biology, psychology, sociology |
| chron- | time | chronological, anachronism |
| phil- | love | philosophy, philanthropy |
| mis- | hate | misanthrope, misogyny |
| loqui- | speak | eloquent, loquacious, colloquial |
| ver- | truth | verify, veracious, verdict |
| cred- | believe | credible, incredulous, credit |
| ven- | come | intervene, convene, convention |

> [!TIP]
> **Rule:** If you don't know a word in the passage AND it's not in a vocabulary-in-context question, SKIP it. Understanding 80% of the passage is enough to answer all questions correctly.

## 1.6 Placement RC vs CAT RC vs GRE RC

Understanding how placement RC differs from competitive exam RC helps you calibrate your effort appropriately.

| Feature | Placement RC (TCS/Infosys) | CAT RC | GRE RC |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Passage Length** | 200-350 words | 400-700 words | 300-500 words (or 1-paragraph) |
| **Questions per Passage** | 3-7 | 4-6 | 1-4 |
| **Difficulty** | Moderate | Very High | High |
| **Inference Depth** | Surface to moderate | Deep, multi-step | Deep |
| **Trap Sophistication** | Moderate | Very High | High |
| **Vocabulary Difficulty** | Moderate | High | Very High |
| **Topic Types** | Business, Tech, General | Economics, Philosophy, Science | Sciences, Humanities, Social Science |
| **True/False/Can't Say** | Very common | Rare | Rare |
| **Time per Question** | ~30-45 seconds | ~60-90 seconds | ~60-75 seconds |
| **Negative Marking** | Usually NO | YES | NO |

### Implication for Strategy

Since placements have **no negative marking** and **moderate difficulty**, your strategy should be:
1. **Attempt all questions** — never leave blank
2. **Prioritize speed** over depth of analysis
3. **True/False/Cannot be determined** questions are placement-specific traps — learn them well
4. **Vocabulary-in-context** questions are easier than GRE — context always helps

> [!NOTE]
> CAT and GRE RC train you for abstract, philosophical, and complex scientific passages. Placement RC focuses on **business, technology, and current affairs** — topics you already have background knowledge in. Use this to your advantage.

## 1.7 Time Allocation Framework

### The Golden Rule of RC Time Management

```
Total RC Time Budget Formula:
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Passage Reading Time  = 60-90 seconds per passage
Per Question Time     = 30-45 seconds
Total Time per Set    = Reading + (Questions × 30s)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────

EXAMPLE: TCS NQT — 10 RC questions in 30 minutes (shared with grammar)
Assume: 2 passages × 5 questions each
Time for grammar (10q): ~12 minutes
Time for RC (10q):      ~18 minutes
Per passage reading:    ~75 seconds
Per question:           ~100 seconds (generous!)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

### Exam-Specific Time Allocation

| Exam | RC Questions | Total Verbal Time | Recommended RC Budget |
|---|---|---|---|
| TCS NQT | 10 | 30 min | 15-18 min |
| Infosys | 15 | 35 min | 20-22 min |
| Cognizant GenC | 8-10 | 25 min | 12-15 min |
| Wipro NLTH | 8-10 | 25 min | 12-14 min |
| Accenture | 5-6 | 20 min | 7-9 min |
| Capgemini | 4-5 | 16 min | 6-8 min |

### Time Management Rules

**Rule 1: The 90-Second Rule for Passages**
Never spend more than 90 seconds reading a passage for the first time. If you're not done, move to questions and return to the passage as needed.

**Rule 2: The 45-Second Rule for Questions**
If you can't answer a question in 45 seconds, mark your best guess and move on. Don't let one question cost you two.

**Rule 3: Sequence Optimization**
Answer questions in this order:
1. Specific detail questions (fastest — just locate the fact)
2. Main idea questions (you know this from recall)
3. Vocabulary-in-context (use context, quick)
4. Inference questions (require more thinking)
5. Tone questions (if unsure, do last)

**Rule 4: The 2-Pass System**
- Pass 1: Answer all questions you can in under 30 seconds
- Pass 2: Return to skipped questions with remaining time

## 1.9 Predicting Answers Before Reading Options

This is perhaps the **most powerful single technique** in RC. Here's why it works:

The human brain in a multiple-choice test is vulnerable to a phenomenon called **"lure of the distractor."** When you read wrong options before knowing the answer, your brain starts entertaining those wrong possibilities. You read: "(A) The author is critical of all technology" and even though it's wrong, a part of your brain starts looking for evidence to confirm it.

**The Solution: Predict First, Then Match**

Before reading options:
1. State your expected answer in your own words
2. Then read options and find the one that best matches

### How to Predict for Each Question Type

**Main Idea Questions:**
- Prediction: Use your 10-second recall summary
- "The passage discusses [topic] and argues that [main point]"

**Specific Detail Questions:**
- Prediction: Locate the relevant paragraph
- "Line 12-14 says [exact info]"

**Inference Questions:**
- Prediction: "Based on the author's claim that X, we can conclude Y"
- The inference must be a LOGICAL EXTENSION, not a leap

**Tone Questions:**
- Prediction: "The author is [positive/negative/neutral] because of [word evidence]"

**Vocabulary-in-Context Questions:**
- Prediction: "This word means approximately [synonym] based on its context"

### The Predict-Match-Confirm System

```
Step 1: Read the question stem
Step 2: Return to the relevant part of the passage
Step 3: Form your prediction in 5-10 words
Step 4: Read all four options
Step 5: Match the option closest to your prediction
Step 6: Confirm by re-reading the passage line
Step 7: Choose and move on
```

> [!WARNING]
> **Never choose an option just because it contains words from the passage.** Wrong options are deliberately constructed with correct vocabulary from the passage but wrong meaning or context. The trap is called "keyword matching trap."

## 1.10 The Scope Test for Eliminating Wrong Answers

The **scope** of a passage refers to the boundaries of what the author discusses, claims, and implies. The most reliable elimination technique in RC is checking whether each answer option **stays within the scope** of the passage.

### Out-of-Scope Violations

**Type 1: Too Broad (Goes Beyond the Passage)**

The passage discusses AI's impact on the banking sector. The question asks for the main idea.
- Wrong option: "AI is transforming every aspect of human civilization."
- Why wrong: The passage only talks about banking. "Every aspect of human civilization" is BEYOND SCOPE.
- Correct option: "AI is revolutionizing customer service and fraud detection in banking."

**Type 2: Too Narrow (Only Part of the Passage)**

The passage discusses remote work, covering benefits, challenges, and policy implications.
- Wrong option: "Remote work leads to increased productivity."
- Why wrong: This is only one point from the passage — NOT the main idea.
- Correct option: "Remote work presents both opportunities and challenges that require thoughtful organizational response."

**Type 3: Contradictory (Opposite of What the Passage Says)**

The passage says AI will augment human jobs, not replace them.
- Wrong option: "AI will make human workers completely obsolete."
- Why wrong: Directly contradicts the passage.

**Type 4: Extreme Language Trap**

Correct passages use qualified language: "may," "often," "tends to," "in many cases."
Wrong options use absolute language: "always," "never," "all," "completely," "universally."

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The Extreme Language Rule:** In RC, any option containing the words **always, never, all, none, completely, universally, inevitably, certainly** is almost certainly WRONG unless the passage itself uses equally extreme language. Placement passages rarely make absolute claims.

### The Scope Test Flowchart

```
For each answer option, ask:
         ↓
1. Does this option mention something the passage NEVER discussed?
         ↓ YES → ELIMINATE (Out of Scope)
         ↓ NO → Continue
2. Does this option CONTRADICT something the passage stated?
         ↓ YES → ELIMINATE (Contradictory)
         ↓ NO → Continue
3. Does this option use EXTREME language the passage doesn't support?
         ↓ YES → ELIMINATE (Extreme)
         ↓ NO → Continue
4. Is this option too BROAD (beyond what passage covers)?
         ↓ YES → ELIMINATE (Too Broad)
         ↓ NO → Continue
5. Is this option too NARROW (only covers one small detail)?
         ↓ YES → Possible, but may not be the MAIN IDEA answer
         ↓ NO → This is likely CORRECT ✅
```

### Common Scope Traps in Placement Exams

| Trap Type | Example | Why Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| New Information | "Studies show AI reduces crime" (not in passage) | Introduces fact not in passage |
| Overgeneralization | "All companies must adopt AI" | Passage says "many," not "all" |
| Value Judgment | "AI is inherently dangerous" | Passage presents balanced view |
| Future Prediction Beyond | "AI will cure cancer" | Passage doesn't say this |
| Partial Truth | "The author is excited about AI" | Passage shows excitement AND concern |

## 1.11 The Mental Model: How Experts Read RC Passages

### The Expert RC Reader's Internal Monologue

Let's walk through what an expert RC reader thinks while reading a passage:

> **Passage (first sentence):** *"The rise of artificial intelligence in corporate environments has fundamentally altered the nature of human work, prompting both excitement and anxiety among economists, policymakers, and workers alike."*

Expert's inner voice:
- *"Okay — topic is AI in corporate environments"*
- *"Key tension: excitement AND anxiety — balanced or conflicted tone likely"*
- *"Stakeholders mentioned: economists, policymakers, workers — author may address multiple perspectives"*
- *"This is likely a 'both sides' passage, not strongly argumentative"*

> **Second sentence:** *"While proponents argue that AI will create new categories of employment, critics warn of widespread displacement in routine-task sectors."*

Expert's inner voice:
- *"Structure confirmed: proponents vs critics — balance confirmed"*
- *"Key claim 1: AI creates new jobs"*
- *"Key claim 2: AI displaces routine jobs"*
- *"Don't take sides yet — author may or may not endorse either view"*

This active engagement continues throughout the passage. By the time the expert reaches the end, they have a structural map:
- P1: Introduction + central tension
- P2: Argument for AI (jobs created)
- P3: Argument against AI (jobs displaced)
- P4: Author's balanced conclusion with call for policy action

**This structural map is built in 60-90 seconds and makes every question 3x easier.**

## 1.12 Full Practice Set — Strategies Applied

### Practice Passage 1

**Topic:** Remote Work and Corporate Culture

> The widespread adoption of remote work, accelerated dramatically by the COVID-19 pandemic, has fundamentally redefined the relationship between employers and employees. What began as an emergency measure has evolved into a permanent fixture for many organizations, particularly in the technology sector. Proponents of remote work cite increased productivity, reduced commute stress, and expanded talent pools as primary benefits. A 2021 Stanford study found that remote workers were 13% more productive than their office counterparts. However, critics argue that remote work erodes the spontaneous collaboration and informal knowledge transfer that office environments naturally facilitate. They also point to the mental health challenges that come with isolation and blurred work-life boundaries. Organizations must therefore develop nuanced policies that balance individual flexibility with collective cohesion, rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach. The future of work, it appears, lies not in choosing between remote and in-office models, but in thoughtfully integrating both.

---

**Q1. What is the central theme of this passage?**

(A) Remote work is more productive than office work.
(B) The COVID-19 pandemic permanently changed corporate culture.
(C) Organizations must balance the benefits and drawbacks of remote work through hybrid policies.
(D) Mental health challenges make remote work unsustainable.

**✅ Answer: (C)**

**📖 Explanation:** The passage presents both benefits (productivity, flexibility) and drawbacks (isolation, loss of collaboration) of remote work and concludes with the need for "nuanced policies" and "thoughtfully integrating both." Option (C) captures this balanced, prescriptive conclusion.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** Too narrow. The passage mentions one study showing 13% productivity gain, but the main idea is not just about productivity. Also, "more productive" is too absolute — the passage qualifies this.

**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** While mentioned in the passage, this is the background context (introductory sentence), not the central theme.

**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** Contradicts the passage's balanced tone. Mental health challenges are mentioned as one concern, but the passage doesn't conclude that remote work is "unsustainable."

**📏 Rule:** Main Idea = The point the ENTIRE passage builds toward, not the first or last sentence alone.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Look for the passage's "therefore" or concluding sentence — it usually states the main idea directly.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Infosys, Wipro NLTH

---

**Q2. According to the passage, which of the following is stated as a benefit of remote work?**

(A) Elimination of workplace politics
(B) Expanded talent pools
(C) Better mental health outcomes
(D) Stronger team cohesion

**✅ Answer: (B)**

**📖 Explanation:** The passage explicitly states "expanded talent pools" as a primary benefit cited by proponents. This is a specific detail question — the answer is directly stated.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** Workplace politics is never mentioned in the passage. Out-of-scope.
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** The passage says remote work creates mental health CHALLENGES (negative), not better outcomes. This is the exact opposite of what the passage says.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** The passage says remote work ERODES team cohesion — again, the opposite.

**📏 Rule:** Specific Detail questions — the answer must be literally stated in the passage. Return and locate.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Eliminate options that contradict the passage or introduce new information.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Accenture, Capgemini

---

**Q3. Which of the following can be inferred from the passage?**

(A) The Stanford study conclusively proves remote work is superior to office work.
(B) Companies that adopt fully remote models will face collaboration issues.
(C) Remote work was always a planned permanent strategy for tech companies.
(D) Employees universally prefer remote work over office work.

**✅ Answer: (B)**

**📖 Explanation:** The passage states remote work "erodes the spontaneous collaboration and informal knowledge transfer that office environments naturally facilitate." A company that goes fully remote would therefore face exactly these collaboration issues. This is a logical extension of the stated fact — a valid inference.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** The word "conclusively proves" is too extreme. The passage says the study "found" a 13% productivity gain — one study finding is not "conclusive proof."
**❌ Why (C) is wrong:** The passage explicitly says remote work "began as an emergency measure" and then evolved. It was NOT always planned as permanent.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** The passage never discusses employee preferences. "Universally prefer" is out of scope AND uses extreme language.

**📏 Rule:** Inference = What MUST be true based on the passage, not what might be true.
**⚡ Shortcut:** Eliminate options with extreme language (conclusively, universally, always, never).
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** Infosys, Cognizant, IBM

**Q4. The author's tone in this passage is best described as:**

(A) Enthusiastically supportive of remote work
(B) Critically dismissive of remote work proponents
(C) Analytically balanced, acknowledging multiple perspectives
(D) Pessimistic about the future of corporate work culture

**✅ Answer: (C)**

**📖 Explanation:** The author presents arguments from both proponents (productivity, flexibility) and critics (isolation, loss of collaboration) without strongly endorsing either side. The conclusion is prescriptive and balanced: "nuanced policies." This is analytical and balanced.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** The author also presents criticisms — not enthusiastically supportive of remote work alone.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** The author presents proponents' views fairly — not dismissive of them.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** The conclusion is constructive ("thoughtfully integrating both"), not pessimistic.

**📏 Rule:** Tone is determined by the OVERALL passage, especially the conclusion.
**⚡ Shortcut:** If the passage presents "both sides + solution," the tone is BALANCED/ANALYTICAL.
**📊 Difficulty:** Medium
**🏢 Company:** TCS NQT, Wipro, Infosys

**Q5. The author suggests that organizations should:**

(A) Immediately adopt fully remote work policies
(B) Return all employees to office environments
(C) Develop flexible policies that integrate both remote and office work
(D) Consult the Stanford study before making any decision

**✅ Answer: (C)**

**📖 Explanation:** The last two sentences directly state: "Organizations must therefore develop nuanced policies that balance individual flexibility with collective cohesion" and "The future of work...lies in thoughtfully integrating both." Option (C) paraphrases this conclusion accurately.

**❌ Why (A) is wrong:** The author explicitly rejects "one-size-fits-all" — fully remote is one extreme.
**❌ Why (B) is wrong:** Returning fully to office is the other extreme — also rejected.
**❌ Why (D) is wrong:** The Stanford study is mentioned as evidence, not as a prescribed decision-making tool.

**📏 Rule:** Purpose/Recommendation questions — look for prescriptive language (should, must, ought to) in the passage.
**⚡ Shortcut:** The author's recommendation is almost always in the LAST paragraph.
**📊 Difficulty:** Easy
**🏢 Company:** All major placement companies

## 📊 Key Takeaways: Chapter 1

| Concept | Core Rule | Remember By |
|---|---|---|
| 4-Step Method | Preview → Read → Recall → Answer | **PRRA** |
| Skimming | First & last sentences of each paragraph | **FLAIR** |
| Scope Test | Eliminate: Too broad, Too narrow, Contradictory, Extreme | **BTCE** |
| Predict First | State answer before reading options | Prevents lure-of-distractor |
| Time Allocation | 60-90s reading, 30-45s per question | Strict budget |
| Main Idea | Entire passage's central argument | Not just first sentence |
| Tone | Determined by word choice + conclusion | Look for adjectives |
| Inference | Must be true, not might be true | Logical extension only |
